Street Fighter Development History, Part 1: The Boring Meeting That Started a Genre (1987)

2026-09-02

A live-action Street Fighter movie is bringing the series back to theaters on October 16, 2026, so we're spending the next five weeks looking back at how the games themselves came to be. The franchise didn't start as a polished fighting game — it started as a doodle passed across a boring meeting table, a giant rubber pad that shredded developers' hands, and a bug that made fireballs nearly impossible to throw. Former Capcom developer Yoshiki Okamoto has spent years telling these stories on his own YouTube channel. This is Part 1: the making of the 1987 arcade original, in his and his colleagues' own words.

  • Released in Japanese arcades in August 1987, directed by Takashi Nishiyama
  • The whole concept began as a hand-drawn sketch passed between coworkers during a dull strategy meeting
  • Special moves fired on button release, not button press — the opposite of every later game
  • Okamoto recalls that shifting unsold stock was one factor behind the six-button layout that became the genre standard

Why Was Street Fighter's Big Idea Born in a Boring Meeting?

The idea for the original Street Fighter wasn't pitched in a design review. It came out of a company strategy meeting so dull that Takashi Nishiyama, sitting next to fellow team leader Yoshiki Okamoto, started sketching on a legal pad just to stay awake. What he drew was a rough concept for two fighters facing off one-on-one, scrawled with a note that amounted to "something like this — I think it could work." Okamoto glanced over, and by his own account his eyes lit up immediately: he asked on the spot to be the one to build it. A hand-passed sketch at a meeting nobody wanted to be in became the genesis of a genre.

Why Was It So Hard to Throw a Fireball in the Original Game?

Anyone who's actually played the 1987 original remembers how stubborn its special moves were, and how devastating a landed Hadouken or Shoryuken felt when it finally connected (a pressure-pad-driven cabinet also shaped that game — we covered its punishing punch pads in our companion piece on the original hardware). Okamoto has explained on his channel that the difficulty came down to a quirk in the game's input code: it checked for a special-move command the moment a button was released, not the moment it was pressed. Under pressure, players instinctively jam the button down and hold it — exactly the one input the game wasn't watching for. By his account, that release-based detection was reworked for Street Fighter II so a command could register on either the press or the release, a change that made the sequel feel dramatically more responsive.

How Did Leftover Inventory Help Push Street Fighter to Six Buttons?

The original Street Fighter shipped in a large, expensive upright cabinet, and it didn't sell as well as Capcom had hoped, leaving warehouses stacked with unsold boards and control panels. The game was later reworked into a cheaper conversion kit built around an 8-way joystick and six buttons — three punches, three kicks — so operators could drop it into cabinets they already owned. The move away from the original cabinet's pressure pads is usually credited to player safety, since the pads were injuring the people who played on them. Okamoto's recollection adds a second, more mundane factor alongside it: leftover stock Capcom needed to move, which by his account helped the six-button version reach arcades as quickly as it did. Whatever the weighting of the two, that layout became the fighting game standard and has stayed there for decades.

Why Did Street Fighter Change How Capcom Wrote Design Documents?

The original Street Fighter's design and programming teams worked out of two different cities, Osaka and Kanazawa, with no fast, reliable way to keep a remote programming team in sync with the design team on the other end. That distance is credited with pushing Capcom to start writing detailed design documents instead of relying on verbal direction, a practice that stuck around long after the original shipped. It's also the backdrop for the series' most famous name: Ryu is named directly after series creator Takashi Nishiyama, using an alternate reading of the first character in "Takashi" (隆). The hero who's carried the franchise for almost 40 years is, quite literally, named after the person who created him.
