Blazing Star on Neo Geo AES: Yumekobo’s First Game, Its Code NGH-2390, and Why There Was No Third Star

2026-11-03

  • Neo Geo
  • Shoot 'em up
  • 1990s
  • SNK
  • Yumekobo
  • Japan Only

Blazing Star is a horizontally scrolling shooter Yumekobo built for SNK, and it is the most expensive object this series has looked at. It went into arcades on 19 January 1998 and came home a month later: on 26 February 1998 the same game went on sale as a Neo Geo AES cartridge, product code NGH-2390, 346 megabits, 29,800円 before tax, the Japanese title spelled ブレイジングスター on the spine.

This is the sixth entry in a series about games that were good enough to earn a second part and never got one. Blazing Star is the case where the second part had already happened. It is itself the follow-up — to Pulstar (1995), made by the same studio under its earlier name — and no third ever came. The studio is listed as defunct in 2001, the same year SNK, which had funded it, filed for bankruptcy. This guide covers what the game does, why it is not called Pulstar 2, what the code on the label means, what a cartridge is worth now (a great deal), and why the shop that has copies is selling you arcade boards.

  • Japanese title: ブレイジングスター, developed by Yumekobo (夢工房), published by SNK
  • Neo Geo AES (home cartridge): 26 February 1998 · product code NGH-2390 · 346 megabits · 29,800円 before tax
  • Arcade (Neo Geo MVS): 19 January 1998 · one of the last big Neo Geo shooters
  • Six pilots, one button that does three things: tap for your shot, tap fast for an alternate shot, or hold for a charge attack that can be released focused or dispersed wide
  • Not Pulstar 2 on purpose: the team treated it as a gaiden rather than a direct sequel, and worked through Pulstar Blast, Zero Hour and The Zenith before settling on the name
  • No Neo Geo CD version: one was planned and never released — the only formats are the cartridge and the arcade board
  • No third one: Yumekobo made two more games and is listed as defunct in 2001; SNK filed for bankruptcy the same year
  • What it is worth: Suruga-ya was offering ￥1,080,000 to buy a copy from you on 20 September 2026, and had none to sell
  • Where the copies actually are: eBay, where 19 of 25 results are this game — but most of them are MVS arcade boards, not the AES cartridge

What is Blazing Star, and what does the charge shot do?

It is a horizontal shooter with six playable craft, and Wikipedia describes the control as one button doing three jobs: you tap it for a standard shot, tap it rapidly for an alternate shot, or hold it for a charge attack that releases “a powerful focused shot which can also be dispersed to cover a large area.” Each pilot’s craft has its own shot type and speed, so which of the six you take changes how that one button behaves for the whole run. Power-ups raise your weaponry, and scoring items are scattered through the stages — some of which only appear once you have cleared a specific wave of enemies, which is what turns a second playthrough into a different game from the first.

What people actually remember is how it looks. The development account calls the graphics “the game’s best selling point”: the artists built detailed textures and animation inside the Neo Geo’s 16-colour-per-sprite limit, at a point in 1998 when the hardware was eight years old. Edge praised the “colorful backgrounds and 3D special effects”. The scores were good rather than universal — Player One gave the arcade version 83% and the Neo Geo version 81%, Super Game Power 5/5, the German Video Games 72%, saying it was not “The Ultimate in Neo Geo graphics” — and the modern re-releases have done better still, with 9/10 from Nintendo Life, 8/10 from Nintendo World Report and 9/10 from Pocket Gamer.

And then there is the English. Retro Gamer and Hardcore Gaming 101 both note that the game is partly remembered for its poor translation, and one line got out of the arcade entirely: the game over message reads “You fail it.” Articles since — including one by the linguist Ben Zimmer — have cited it as the origin of the interjection “Fail.” A Neo Geo shooter with a print run this small put a word into English.

Why is this not called Pulstar 2?

Because the studio had just changed its name and wanted the game to stand on its own.

The company was founded as Aicom in 1988. It spent the Famicom and NES years making other publishers’ games — Magic John for Jaleco in 1990, released in North America as Totally Rad; Vice: Project Doom; The Mafat Conspiracy; Blaster Master Boy on Game Boy — and in 1995 it made Pulstar for the Neo Geo. Sammy Corporation bought Aicom in 1992. Then, as Wikipedia puts it, “Aicom broke off from Sammy in 1996 and, with funding from SNK, became Yumekobo,” working on SNK hardware from then on.

Blazing Star was the first game released under the new name, and the team treated it as a gaiden — a side story — rather than a numbered sequel. The Japanese Wikipedia article puts the relationship precisely: it inherits the setting and some of the systems of Pulstar, “but is basically a completely different game.” Before they settled on Blazing Star the candidate titles included Pulstar Blast, Zero Hour and The Zenith. Pulstar Blast would have tied it to the older game; they did not use it.

So the pairing is real but deliberately loose, and it matters when you go shopping: Pulstar and Blazing Star turn up in each other’s search results constantly, and they are different games from different years on different formats — Pulstar also exists on Neo Geo CD, which Blazing Star never did.

Which release is the Japanese cartridge, and what is its code?

NGH-2390, and the last digit trips people up.

Early Neo Geo home cartridges carry three-digit codes — Magician Lord is NGH-005, Top Hunter is NGH-046. By the late 1990s SNK had moved to four digits: MAME’s own data gives The Last Blade NGH-2340 in 1997, Real Bout Fatal Fury 2 NGM-2400, Metal Slug 2 NGM-2410 ~ NGH-2410, The King of Fighters ’98 NGH-2420, and Garou: Mark of the Wolves NGM-2530 ~ NGH-2530 in 1999. Blazing Star is game number 239, released in February 1998, and its code sits in that run as NGH-2390 — between 2340 and 2400.

You will also see NGH-239 written down, and it is worth knowing where that comes from. The Neo Geo Master List prints its catalogue numbers as three digits throughout — it shows Garou as 253 and Metal Slug 2 as 241, whose real codes are NGH-2530 and NGH-2410. The three-digit form is that list’s house style, not the code on the box. The four-digit form is what the shops and the collector databases record: Suruga-ya’s 型番 field reads NGH-2390, the Japanese catalogue Pico Pico Daihyakka gives 型番：NGH-2390, and VGCollect lists item number NGH-2390 alongside the barcode T4964808101214. One caveat we owe you: we could not find a photograph of the printed code at a resolution where it can be read, so this rests on the documents and the number sequence, not on a picture of the label.

The catalog rows themselves carry no code at all. Kataban tracks this game through the Agency for Cultural Affairs Media Arts Database — row M755752 for the cartridge, M728770 for the arcade original, M755751 for the Wii Virtual Console release — and none of them has a schema:productID or a schema:gtin.

Two dates and one absence are worth fixing in your head. The arcade release is 19 January 1998 and the AES cartridge is 26 February 1998: the database, Suruga-ya, Pico Pico Daihyakka and both the Japanese and English Wikipedia articles agree on the February date, against 19 February in the Master List. The Master List also leaves the English AES column empty, which matches what the English Wikipedia says about the disc: a Neo Geo CD version was planned and never released. There is no official overseas AES cartridge and no CD. The 1998 Japanese cartridge is the only home release of this game that exists as an object.

What did it cost, and what is it worth now?

It cost 29,800円 before tax, and this time the sources agree: Suruga-ya’s 定価 field reads ￥32,780, which is 29,800 × 1.1 at today’s rate. That was a lot in 1998 — more than a games console — and 346 megabits is part of why. It is one of the largest Neo Geo cartridges ever made, and the Master List and the Media Arts Database record the same figure.

Now it is in a different category of expensive. On 20 September 2026, Suruga-ya’s trade-in price — what the shop will pay you for a copy — was ￥1,080,000, and the shop had nothing to sell. That number is not an outlier: the Japanese buyer 中古ゲーム買取.com quotes ￥905,000 for a complete copy with box and manual. Two independent buyers in the same country are both offering around a million yen.

On the international market the asking prices are all over the place, and we are going to list them without telling you what they mean. On the same day, eBay’s listings for this game ranged from a “complete in box” AES cartridge at about ￥42,500 to one at about ￥2,040,000, with the arcade boards clustering near ￥100,000. A cartridge that two Japanese shops will pay ￥900,000-plus for, offered at ￥42,500, is a gap we cannot explain from here — and 中古ゲーム買取.com’s own page carries a warning that counterfeits of this title are circulating. We are not in a position to judge any individual listing, so we are not going to. Read the seller, the photographs and the return policy yourself.

Why was there never a third one?

Because the studio stopped existing, and so did the company paying for it.

Yumekobo released two more games after Blazing Star — Puzzle Link and SNK Gals’ Fighters — and Wikipedia lists the company as defunct in 2001, with SNK named as its successor. SNK’s own situation that year is a matter of record: it filed for bankruptcy in 2001. We are putting those two facts next to each other rather than joining them with a “because”, since the sources do not spell out the mechanism. What is not in doubt is the result. The studio that made Pulstar in 1995 and Blazing Star in 1998 was gone within three years of the second one, and the funder it had broken away from Sammy to work with was gone at the same time.

What the game got instead was re-releases, and fewer than most of this series: the Wii Virtual Console on 21 August 2012 at 926Wiiポイント, published by D4 Enterprise, and Hamster’s ACA NEOGEO edition on 2 May 2017 at 823円 including tax. A shooter that critics scored in the eighties and nineties, that put a phrase into the English language, and that now trades for the price of a car — and there is no Blazing Star 2.

Can you actually buy the AES cartridge today?

Yes, but almost certainly not the one you are picturing, and not from Japan.

Suruga-ya, 20 September 2026: nothing. The shop’s own stock of the cartridge (product 163000193) was 品切れ — sold out. The search results do show a third-party copy at ￥408,000, but opening that listing gives the same message: 品切れ中. There is no route to a copy through Suruga-ya today, only the ￥1,080,000 trade-in offer sitting there.

Buyee, the same day: nothing at all, and this is worth stating plainly because it is the site this series normally sends you to. The Japanese title with the platform, ブレイジングスター ネオジオ, returns seven listings and not one of them is this game — they are a HyperMegaTech Super Pocket, ACA NEOGEO compilation cartridges for Switch, and Neo Geo Minis. The bare title returns twenty-seven, and the game is not in those either: four soundtrack CDs, one issue of Neo Geo Freak from August 1998, three compilations, and then Tamiya radio-control buggies (there is an RC car called Blazing Star), Touhou Project trading cards and a Vanguard card. One listing reading ブレイジングスター(中古品) looks promising until you open it and find it filed under Auctions > Music > CD. The product code returns nothing in any spelling. There is no Neo Geo cartridge for this game on Buyee.

eBay, the same day: this is where the copies are. Blazing Star Neo Geo returned 25 results, of which 19 are this game — but read what they are. Fourteen are MVS arcade boards, the cartridge that goes into a coin-op, which will not run in an AES console without an adapter. Two are described by their own sellers as conversions: “MVS-to-AES mask ROM conversion”, “AES - Blazing Star (conversion)”. Only three describe themselves as AES cartridges, and one of those calls itself a “US English AES” — which cannot be an official release, because there was never an English AES version of this game. The remaining six results are a snap case, a replacement box, two soundtrack CDs, an Evercade cartridge and a mug.

So what can you actually get? If you want to play it, the honest answer is the ACA NEOGEO release, which is on current hardware for 823円 and is the same game. If you want an arcade board, eBay has a dozen and they cost about a tenth of what a cartridge does. If you specifically want the 1998 AES cartridge with NGH-2390 on it, you are looking at a market with three listings worldwide, a price range spanning a factor of fifty, and a known counterfeit problem. That is a watch-and-learn purchase, not a click-and-buy one.

How do you search each shop for it?

Suruga-ya: use the four-digit code. NGH2390 returns one listing and it is this cartridge, with sold-out items shown or not. So does NGH-2390 — unusually, the hyphen makes no difference here, because Suruga-ya matches from the start of the field and both spellings reach the stored value. The three-digit NGH239 also works, but NGH-239 with sold-out items shown pulls in トッププレイヤーズゴルフ as well. The clean search is the full code, and the Japanese title ブレイジングスター is not much worse: two results, one of them this game and the other the ACA NEOGEO compilation.

Buyee: do not bother. Measured above: zero. If you want to watch for a copy appearing there, watch the title ブレイジングスター and expect to scroll past radio-control cars.

eBay: use the English title with the platform. Blazing Star Neo Geo is the search — 25 results, 19 of them this game. The product code works too, and works precisely: NGH-2390 returned exactly one result and it was an AES cartridge. But one result is one seller, and the code search will go empty the moment that copy sells, which is why the title search is the link in this guide.

Read the titles, not the counts. The words that decide what you are buying are the format ones: MVS or board or arcade is the coin-op cartridge, AES is the home cartridge, conversion or MVS-to-AES means an arcade board rebuilt into a home shell, softbox and snap case are replacement packaging with no game in them, and OST or soundtrack is a music CD. Note also that Pulstar appears constantly in Blazing Star searches and the reverse — sellers list them together because of the connection described above.

Why search Kataban for this one?

Because both names work, which is not always true. Checked in a desktop browser on 20 September 2026, ブレイジングスター returns two results and Blazing Star returns the same two: the 1998 Neo Geo cartridge and the 2012 Wii Virtual Console release. Both database rows carry the English title, so you can come at this game from either language — unlike Top Hunter earlier in this series, where the English title returns nothing.

Two is also the whole home history, which is the useful part. There is no Neo Geo CD row because there is no Neo Geo CD version, and the arcade board is not a home release. If you are reading a listing and trying to work out which of several things you are looking at, the catalog’s answer is that there are only two, and one of them is a download.

The product code is a way in as of today. The database rows carry no code of their own, so the field was filled in here from the sources named above — and because the catalog strips punctuation before it matches, NGH-2390 and NGH2390 both land on the cartridge. On a Japanese shop shelf, the kana to look under is フ.

A studio that spent the Famicom years making other people’s games, broke away from its owner, took SNK’s money, and spent 346 megabits on a shooter for a machine that was already eight years old. It got the graphics it wanted and a mistranslation that outlived the company. Three years later the studio was gone and so was SNK. If you want the object, it is the cartridge with NGH-2390 on it, and the market for it is three listings and a warning about fakes. If you want the game, it is 823円 on a Switch.
