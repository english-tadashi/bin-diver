Castlevania Development History, Part 1: The Disk That Vanished and the Cartridge That Replaced It

2026-09-09

Castlevania started life in 1986 on a format that no longer physically survives in good numbers: the Famicom Disk System. Konami's original 悪魔城ドラキュラ shipped on a rewritable magnetic disk, and the way those disks were sold — and repeatedly overwritten — is the reason the game most Japanese collectors actually buy today is not the 1986 original at all, but a cartridge Konami put out seven years later. Part 1 covers the Famicom and Game Boy era, and why the product code on the box matters more here than in almost any other series.

  • The 1986 original was a Famicom Disk System title, sold on rewritable disks that customers could overwrite with a different game for 500 yen
  • Konami re-released it as a standard ROM cartridge in 1993 with product code RV003 — this is the version that actually circulates on the used market
  • Castlevania II (KDS-DRK, 1987) stayed on disk; Castlevania III (RC845, 1989) moved to cartridge and carried a custom sound chip
  • The Japanese cartridge of Castlevania III contains Konami's VRC6 audio chip, which the Western NES release did not have — the same game sounds different depending on which region's cartridge you own
  • Two Game Boy entries followed: DMG-CVJ (1989) and DMG-CWJ (1991)

Why Is the 1986 Original So Hard to Find in Good Condition?

悪魔城ドラキュラ launched on 26 September 1986 as a Famicom Disk System title. The Disk System was Nintendo's answer to cartridge costs: games shipped on a proprietary rewritable magnetic disk, and rather than buying a new copy outright, customers could take a disk they already owned to a Disk Writer kiosk in a shop and have a different game written onto it for 500 yen. It was an elegant solution to an expensive problem, and it was devastating for long-term preservation. Every disk overwritten with a new game is one fewer surviving copy of whatever was on it before. Nintendo ran the rewriting service until 30 September 2003, so the overwriting went on for seventeen years.

The format worked against the game in a second way. Magnetic media degrades. Disk System disks in circulation today are decades past the point where the medium can be assumed readable, and a disk that boots at the shop may not boot next year. Original labels wear, and a rewritten disk carries no reliable trace of what it started as. So the practical situation for a buyer is this: 1986 copies exist, they are not cheap, and condition is genuinely unpredictable in a way that has nothing to do with how carefully the previous owner treated it.

The 1986 disk does have a product code: its label reads KDS-AKM, in the same KDS- series as Castlevania II's KDS-DRK. What is missing is the catalog's record of it. The Kataban catalog holds codes for some of Konami's Disk System titles, KDS-DRK among them, but the row for the 1986 original is blank in that column. Searching Suruga-ya for KDS-AKM does bring up the disk version.

What Is RV003 and Why Does It Matter?

On 5 February 1993, Konami re-released 悪魔城ドラキュラ on the Famicom as a standard ROM cartridge, with the product code RV003. Nearly seven years after the original, on hardware that was already being superseded, the first Castlevania became a normal cartridge you could put on a shelf and expect to still work.

That cartridge is what the used market is actually made of. Search Suruga-ya for RV003 and the listings come back labelled explicitly as the ROM cartridge version — the shop itself distinguishes it from the disk. If you want a copy of the first Castlevania that will boot reliably, that is the one to look for, and searching by product code is the fastest way to avoid landing on a disk listing by accident.

It is worth being clear about what you are buying, though. RV003 is a 1993 re-release. If what you want is the artifact — the thing that shipped in 1986, in the sleeve it shipped in — the cartridge is not a substitute for it. It is the practical copy, not the historical one.

How Did Castlevania II and III Split Across Two Formats?

ドラキュラ2 呪いの封印 followed in 1987, product code KDS-DRK, and stayed on the Disk System. Its structure differs from the first game's: instead of a fixed sequence of stages, it uses a connected overworld of towns and mansions that the player can move through in either direction. A day and night cycle runs on a timer, changing enemy placement and closing the buildings in town until morning. Villagers supply progress information as text lines when spoken to. Because it is a Disk System title, it saves to the disk itself, and it carries the same format-related condition problems as the original.

悪魔城伝説 arrived in 1989 as RC845, and this time on cartridge. It makes the route through the game non-linear: at certain points the player chooses between two paths, and there are three such crossroads and six possible paths in total. Trevor Belmont can also be joined by one of three companions — Sypha Belnades, Grant Danasty, or Alucard — one at a time. But the reason collectors care about the Japanese cartridge specifically is the hardware inside it.

Why Does the Japanese Castlevania III Sound Different?

The RC845 cartridge contains Konami's VRC VI (VRC6), a custom chip that adds two pulse channels and one sawtooth channel to the Famicom's standard five, for eight in total. The Western NES release of the same game could not use it, and the reason is in the cartridge connector itself.

The Famicom's cartridge port is 60-pin and carries two dedicated lines for cartridge-internal audio. The NES port is 72-pin, physically larger and incompatible, and those two audio lines were removed. The pins were reallocated instead — ten to standard cartridge wiring, and four to the 10NES anti-piracy lockout chip. With no path for a cartridge to feed audio back into the console, Konami's chip had nowhere to send its output. The Western version used Nintendo's MMC5 instead, and the soundtrack was rewritten to match.

The result is that Castlevania III is genuinely a different listening experience depending on which region's cartridge is in the machine, and the Japanese one is the version with the fuller score. For a series whose music is a large part of its reputation, that is not a footnote. It is one of the clearest cases in the Famicom library where the product code on the label tells you something substantive about what you will actually hear.

What About the Game Boy Entries?

Konami brought the series to the Game Boy twice in this period. ドラキュラ伝説 (DMG-CVJ, 1989) came first, and it drops the sub-weapon system the Famicom games used. The whip is the only weapon: an orb from a destroyed candle upgrades it to a longer chain whip, and a second orb adds a fireball attack. Hearts, which were sub-weapon ammunition in the Famicom games, restore health here instead. ドラキュラ伝説2 (DMG-CWJ, 1991) brings the sub-weapon system back and changes the structure: its first four levels can be played in any order, and the player travels through five castles in total.

Both are cartridge releases, which means both are straightforward to buy in working condition — the opposite of the situation with the two Disk System entries. If you are assembling the Famicom and Game Boy era of the series, the Game Boy half is the easy half.
