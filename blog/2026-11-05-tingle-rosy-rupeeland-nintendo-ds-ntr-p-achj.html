Freshly-Picked Tingle’s Rosy Rupeeland: the West Disliked Him, Japan Loved Him, and We Think His DS Game Is a Masterpiece

2026-11-05

  • Nintendo DS
  • RPG
  • 2000s
  • Nintendo

Few characters split opinion like Tingle. Outside Japan, the 35-year-old man who wants to be a fairy is one of the most disliked figures in The Legend of Zelda. In Japan, he starred in his own games. The first of them is Freshly-Picked Tingle’s Rosy Rupeeland, and here at Kataban we think it is a masterpiece. That is our opinion, and we will keep it apart from the facts: the scores below do not all agree with us.

If the look of it has always put you off, this guide is for you. It sets out how the two audiences saw him, what the reviews really said, why we disagree with the lower ones, and how to buy the Japanese copy. The timing helps: The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time reaches Switch 2 on 5 November 2026 (official store page, overview trailer), and Tingle’s story starts one game after it.

  • Japanese title: もぎたてチンクルのばら色ルッピーランド, Nintendo DS, Nintendo, developed by Vanpool
  • Product code: NTR-P-ACHJ; barcode 4902370513875
  • Released: Japan 2 September 2006; Europe 14 September 2007; never in North America
  • Reviews: Famitsu Gold Hall of Fame in Japan; 6 to 7.7 out of 10 and 76% in the West
  • Our opinion: Kataban considers it a masterpiece; our reasons are in their own section below
  • Region: it is a standard DS card, and DS cards are not region-locked
  • Language: treat the Japanese card as Japanese only; the European release is the one localised for Western readers
  • Buying it: search もぎたてチンクルのばら色ルッピーランド DS on Buyee, or Tingle Rosy Rupeeland Japan DS on eBay

Why did the West turn on Tingle?

The dislike is well documented. CBR calls him “one of the most infamously unpopular Zelda characters in America”. English Wikipedia records an overwhelmingly negative reception outside Japan, with GamesRadar+ comparing him to Jar Jar Binks and IGN running a “Die, Tingle, Die!” campaign. AUTOMATON says the view that he is disliked remains deep-rooted in America.

Two reasons come up most. One is the character himself, a grown man acting like a child. The other is The Wind Waker, where he stands in the way of the Triforce quest and charges you rupees for it. Even his creator has doubts: in 2022 Takaya Imamura wrote that he had been aiming for “cute-creepy”, and that looking at Tingle now, he was just creepy.

Why does Japan see him differently?

CBR describes him as “one of the most beloved in Japan”, and AUTOMATON says opinions disliking him are rarely seen there. The clearest evidence is commercial. TheGamer counts three spin-off games in Japan, “all of which were successful”. Rosy Rupeeland sold 207,525 copies there in 2006, according to English Wikipedia, and reached 234,862 by the end of 2007.

North America never got it. English Wikipedia says only that it was not released there, and Game Informer wrote in 2018 that Nintendo of America declined. CBR puts that down to American opinion of Tingle; we found no statement from Nintendo itself. Europe got the game. The sequel, いろづきチンクルの恋のバルーントリップ (Tingle’s Balloon Trip of Love, August 2009), stayed in Japan.

So is the game actually good?

In Japan, clearly yes. In the West, the critics were more cautious than the fans. Here is the record:

  • Famitsu (Japan): Gold Hall of Fame, the band for 32 to 34 out of 40, according to Japanese Wikipedia’s category; we could not confirm the exact score
  • Japanese reception: Japanese Wikipedia says magazine and web reviews were generally high; the Japanese Game Catalog wiki rates it a good game, “surprisingly solid” for how it looks
  • Official Nintendo Magazine: 76%
  • VGChartz: 7.7 out of 10; the reviewer put it in their top 10 DS games
  • Nintendo Life and Nintendo World Report: 7 out of 10 each
  • Cubed3: 6 out of 10
  • Aggregates: GameRankings 66.25% from 16 reviews; Metacritic 67, as shown on 4Gamer

We found no industry award for it. What the record shows is a gap: Japan scored it higher than Western outlets did. Some who expected little came round: Zelda Dungeon’s writer says it arrived with a bad reputation and “crushed every expectation I had of it”.

What is actually in the game?

Money is your health. Nintendo Life, Cubed3 and VGChartz all describe the same rule: rupees are Tingle’s life, and if he runs out, he dies. They are also the way forward. A listless 35-year-old is promised paradise by Uncle Rupee, and has to feed rupees into a pool to grow a tower towards Rupeeland; each time it grows, more of the map opens. So every rupee you bank to make progress is a rupee that is no longer keeping you alive.

Fights run on the same sum. Touch an enemy and a cartoon dust cloud appears; you tap it to help Tingle while your rupees drain. You can drag more enemies into the cloud, which costs more but pays better, as both Cubed3 and VGChartz explain. You can also hire one of the bodyguards, thirty in all according to Nintendo Life, each with a different temperament, and pay them again when they tire.

Around that sits an economy. You mark landmarks on the touch screen to make maps and sell them, cook ingredients from recipes into food worth more than the raw goods, and pay for items and information. It has faults: Nintendo Life found the ingredient hunting repetitive, the haggling is guesswork, as both Nintendo Life and VGChartz complain, and Official Nintendo Magazine criticised the dungeons and battle system. Vanpool developed it, with Taro Kudo as director and Kensuke Tanabe as producer.

Kataban’s view: this is a masterpiece

This section is our opinion, not a reported fact. We think Rosy Rupeeland is a masterpiece, even though most of the Western scores above stop at 7.

Here is why. Most role-playing games keep your health and your wallet apart, so spending is painless. This one joins them, and everything else follows: every purchase, every fight and every deposit in the pool is the same question, how much of your life can you afford to give away? Greed in the dust cloud can kill you. Saving too hard stalls the tower. A bodyguard is a real expense, not a menu option. We find few games that turn one rule into so many decisions.

The theme is the other reason. A game about a tired 35-year-old giving up his life to chase money, in the costume of a bright cartoon, is funnier and sharper than it looks, and it reads differently if you are 35 yourself.

We think it will suit players who enjoy budgets, trading and shop-keeping games, players who like comedy with an edge, and Zelda fans who want to see that world from the side of someone with no sword and no destiny. If you need polished dungeons or fair haggling, the faults above will bother you.

And the scores? We suspect first impressions cost it points in the West. Reviewers met a character many already disliked, and the look of the game says little about how careful its sums are. That is our reading, not a finding: we found no critic who says so.

What does Tingle have to do with Ocarina of Time?

One step removed. Tingle is not in the original Ocarina of Time: his first appearance is its direct sequel, The Legend of Zelda: Majora’s Mask (Nintendo 64, Japan, 27 April 2000), where he sells Link maps. English Wikipedia describes him as fixated on the Kokiri, the forest fairies. We found nothing saying whether the Switch 2 game adds him.

Japanese copy or European copy?

Here is what we could confirm:

  • Date: Japan 2 September 2006; Europe 14 September 2007
  • Game ID: ACHJ for Japan, ACHP for Europe (GameTDB); on Japanese shop pages the full code is NTR-P-ACHJ
  • Title: Freshly-Picked Tingle’s Rosy Rupeeland in English and German; French, Italian and Spanish each have their own title, listed by Zelda Wiki
  • Rating: CERO A in Japan; PEGI 12 in Europe and USK 6 in Germany

We found no reliable list of gameplay changes between the two, so we are not claiming there are any, or that there are none. Nintendo Life reviewed the European version and does not mention any.

So the choice is about language. The game leans on text, and you will be haggling in it. If you want to read every line in English, the European copy is the one that was localised. If you read Japanese, want the original release, or are collecting the Japanese Tingle games together, get the Japanese copy. For readers in North America, both are imports.

Will a Japanese card run on your DS?

Yes. Our Fire Emblem DS guide covers the rule in full: a standard DS card with a code beginning NTR is region-free on a DS or DS Lite. The lock on DSi and 3DS systems applies to DSi-enhanced and DSi-exclusive software, and this game came out in 2006 as a standard card, so the Japanese copy should start on those systems as well.

Where can you buy it today?

On 31 October 2026 we counted 16 single copies of the game among 28 listings on Buyee.

Three things will get in your way. Suruga-ya spells the title with katakana, バラ色, not ばら色, so the code is the safer search there. Some sellers on Buyee write ハッピーランド by mistake. And both Buyee and eBay mix in strategy guides, bundles with other Tingle games and, on eBay, a case and manual with no game: read the listing before you bid.

On eBay the plain English title mostly finds European copies, so add Japan if you want the Japanese one.
