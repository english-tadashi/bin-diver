Ghosts 'n Goblins on Famicom: The Home Port That Made the Arcade Look Easy

2026-09-06

Capcom's Ghosts 'n Goblins is on sale on Steam right now as part of Capcom Arcade Stadium's bundle discount — a good excuse to revisit the game's brutal 1986 Famicom port, which somehow made an already punishing arcade game even harder.

  • What it is: A side-scrolling action game where knight Arthur loses his armor (then his life) in two hits, fighting through undead hordes to rescue a princess
  • Arcade original: 1985, Capcom
  • Famicom port: June 13, 1986 — developed by Micronics, published by Capcom
  • Why the Famicom version is notable: It's widely considered harder than the arcade original, with slower weapons and stricter timing, but it adds extra hidden characters not in the arcade release
  • Western release: NES as Ghosts 'n Goblins (PAL Europe used Ghost 'n Goblins, singular)
  • Product code to search for: CAP-MK

Why is the Famicom version harder than the arcade original?

Capcom outsourced the Famicom port to Micronics, and the result trimmed Arthur's weapon speed and tightened enemy timing to fit the hardware. Japanese retrospectives marking the port's 40th anniversary this June singled it out as tougher than the coin-op it came from — a reputation that's followed the cartridge for decades.

What do Japanese fans mean by “鬼畜ゲー” and “死にゲー”?

Japanese players have two well-worn terms for punishingly hard games like this one. 鬼畜ゲー (kichiku-ge, literally “devil/brute game”) describes a game that feels almost sadistically unfair — cheap hits, tight timing, no room for error. 死にゲー (shinige, “death game”) is broader: a game built around repeated failure, where dying over and over is how you're expected to learn the patterns, not a sign you're bad at it. Ghosts 'n Goblins — and especially its Famicom port — gets called both. Japanese retrospectives on the cartridge routinely use kichiku to describe the Micronics port's stricter timing, while shinige captures the whole series' design philosophy: two full loops just to see the real ending, and dying is simply part of the routine.

Why has the Red Arremer earned such a notorious reputation as a “fiend from hell”?

Ask players in Japan and the West which enemy in Ghosts 'n Goblins they loathe most, and you'll likely get the same answer. It takes three hits to bring down, spits fireballs from the air, and — if the fight drags on past a minute — can even transform into a white variant that's far more aggressive. Language barrier aside, players on both sides of the Pacific reach for the same verdict on the Red Arremer: it is the ultimate fiend.

Is the Famicom version worth playing next to the arcade one?

If you've been enjoying Ghosts 'n Goblins through Capcom Arcade Stadium, the Famicom cartridge is a different, harder game built around the same story — reach the ending twice to see it through, same as the arcade. It also has hidden characters absent from the arcade release, which retro-game guides list as one of its few advantages over the original.
