Street Fighter Development History, Part 5: The Rule the Old Guard Couldn't Break

2026-09-29

In Part 4, we covered a stock-clearance rush job, a leaked technology secret, a mass defection to a rival, and a comedy character that nearly caused an international incident with Marvel. For the finale of this series, timed to the October 2026 movie release, we're jumping forward three decades to the newest entry in the franchise, Street Fighter 6, and a change its own creators say they wouldn't have had the nerve to make.

  • For over 30 years, fighting games demanded six buttons and precise, memorized command inputs — a barrier that kept the genre's audience aging in place
  • Yoshiki Okamoto, one of the people who built those very rules, says that if he'd stayed at Capcom he never would have approved breaking them
  • Street Fighter 6's Modern control scheme cuts inputs to four buttons and adds one-button special moves and assisted combos
  • Okamoto argues Modern controls actually favor certain classic characters at the highest levels of competitive play

What Unspoken Rule Had Fighting Games Followed for 30 Years?

By the time Street Fighter 6 was in development, the fighting game genre had quietly settled into an assumption nobody questioned: that a fighting game meant six buttons split between punches and kicks, and combos built from precise, memorized command inputs that took real practice to execute. That assumption rewarded players who'd been building muscle memory for decades and made the genre a difficult one for anyone new to break into, which left its competitive scene skewing older as newer players bounced off the learning curve.

Why Didn't Okamoto Think He Could Have Made This Change Himself?

The system that finally broke that assumption is Street Fighter 6's Modern control scheme, and Yoshiki Okamoto — one of the developers who helped establish the genre's traditional rules in the first place — has said plainly on his own channel that he wouldn't have had the nerve to approve it. Having personally shaped the original six-button layout and the command-input system it depended on, he's said he was too invested in the rules he'd built to be the one to tear them down. Credit for the decision, in his telling, belongs to a newer generation of Capcom developers who joined the company after the Street Fighter II boom had already passed into history — young enough that they weren't carrying the weight of that era's conventions, and willing to break rules that Okamoto's generation had never seriously questioned.

What Does Modern Control Actually Change?

The redesign is straightforward on paper: six buttons collapse into three attack buttons plus one dedicated special-move button, letting a player fire off a Hadouken or Shoryuken with a single press instead of a joystick motion. An assist option lets combos connect automatically while mashing an attack button, rather than requiring the precise timing classic mode demands. Between them, the two changes let two players of very different skill levels have a competitive, entertaining match without either one needing years of practice first.

What Are Modern Controls' Real Weaknesses?

Okamoto doesn't treat Modern as a pure beginner's cheat code, and he's been specific on his channel about its costs. Collapsing six buttons into three roughly halves the number of distinct normal and special attacks a character can throw out, which narrows a player's options enough that high-level opponents can read and counter their patterns more easily. Charge characters like Chun-Li, who rely on holding a direction before releasing a special move, also translate poorly to the scheme and are, in his words, a genuinely rough experience to play in Modern.

Where Could Modern Controls Actually Beat Classic at the Highest Level?

Okamoto expects Classic controls to remain dominant at the top of competitive play, by his estimate in 80 to 90 percent of high-level matches. But he sees one scenario where Modern could flip a match even at a world championship level: characters like Zangief and E. Honda, whose signature moves traditionally require a full 360-degree or double joystick rotation to execute. In Modern, that same move fires instantly off a single button, cutting out the fraction of a second a full rotation costs and the input errors that come with it — a real competitive edge in exactly the matchups where the old system was hardest to execute under pressure.

Okamoto sums up his hope for the mode simply: widen the base of players who can get into fighting games, and the pool of genuinely great players at the top gets deeper too. He's said he still personally prefers the feeling of manually landing a combo the hard way, the way his own generation grew up playing — but he's also said that preference might just be an old habit the genre no longer needs, and that he'd be happy if, decades from now, a new generation of players packing arenas for Street Fighter tournaments paused for a moment to wonder who actually made this game in the first place.
