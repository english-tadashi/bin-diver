Viewpoint on Neo Geo AES: the Hand-Drawn Isometric Shooter, and the Sequel That Arrived 26 Years Late

2026-11-04

  • Neo Geo
  • Shoot 'em up
  • 1990s
  • Sammy
  • Aicom

Viewpoint is a scrolling shooter Aicom built for Sammy, and it is the only Neo Geo game Sammy ever put its name on. It reached arcades in October 1992, and on 11 December 1992 the same game went on sale as a Neo Geo AES cartridge, product code NGH-051, 72 megabits, 25,000円 before tax, the Japanese title spelled ビューポイント on the spine.

This is the seventh entry in a series about games that were good enough to earn a second part and never got one. Viewpoint is the entry where that stopped being true — not because a sequel was released, but because one was announced, cancelled, and then, twenty-six years after its planned release, dumped onto the internet by preservationists. It is also the third time this series has run into the same studio: Aicom made this in 1992, Pulstar in 1995, and then, renamed Yumekobo, Blazing Star in 1998. This guide covers how the game got its look, what the code on the label means, what it costs now, where to buy it — and what happened in November 2025.

  • Japanese title: ビューポイント, developed by Aicom, published by Sammy with SNK
  • Neo Geo AES (home cartridge): 11 December 1992 · product code NGH-051 · 72 megabits · 25,000円 before tax
  • Arcade (Neo Geo MVS): October 1992 · Sammy’s only Neo Geo release
  • Neo Geo CD: 25 February 1995 · product code AICD-051 · reprogrammed by Aicom
  • Hand-drawn 3D: the isometric graphics were drawn by hand — no pre-rendering software, no 3D hardware
  • Three bombs, six stages: a fire wave that rolls up the screen from behind you, a circular nova blast, and homing mini-missiles
  • The sequel was cancelled: Viewpoint 2064 was announced for the Nintendo 64 for 11 November 1999, delayed through 2000, and abandoned in 2001
  • And then it wasn’t: on 24 November 2025 a final build of Viewpoint 2064 was dumped and uploaded to the Internet Archive
  • What it costs: Suruga-ya had no cartridge on 20 September 2026 and was offering ￥180,000 for one — but the Neo Geo CD version was on the shelf at ￥6,370

What is Viewpoint, and why does it look like that?

It is a shooter seen from a corner. Wikipedia traces the lineage directly: the “isometric-scrolling gameplay was largely inspired by Sega’s Zaxxon”, with aesthetic influences from Xevious and R-Type. You fly across a grid rather than straight down it, which means every enemy pattern has to be read on two axes at once, and the shadow under your ship is as important as the ship.

Beyond the standard shot there are three bombs, each doing something different with that geometry: one sends a wave of fire rolling up the screen from behind you, one detonates as a circular nova blast, and one launches homing mini-missiles. There are six stages. Two players normally alternate turns, though simultaneous play can be switched on in Service Mode — which is worth knowing if you are buying an arcade board rather than a cartridge.

The part worth stopping on is how the graphics were made. This is 1992, the year everyone was starting to talk about pre-rendered CG, and Wikipedia is explicit: “The game’s 3D graphics were drawn entirely by hand, as opposed to using pre-rendering software or 3D hardware.” Every one of those chrome spheres and segmented insects was drawn frame by frame by a person. The soundtrack is by Sizlla Okamura.

The reviews at the time were extraordinary. GameFan scored it 99.5% and 99% and called it “the best game on the system so far”. GamePro gave it perfect fives across every category. Computer and Video Games gave 95%, Sinclair User 96%, and Famitsu 30/40. The later home conversions did not go as well — the Genesis and PlayStation versions drew mixed reviews for their technical compromises and controls, with IGN giving the PlayStation port 2/10 and reviewers calling it “unbelievably hard”. If you have only ever played Viewpoint on a PlayStation disc, you have not really played it.

Who made it, and why is Sammy’s name on only one Neo Geo game?

Aicom made it. The studio was founded in 1988, and Wikipedia’s development section notes it was known for The Legendary Axe and The Astyanax. Regular readers of this site will have met it before: Aicom developed Magic John for Jaleco on the Famicom in 1990, the game that became Totally Rad in North America. It went on to make Pulstar for the Neo Geo in 1995, and in 1996 it broke away from Sammy, took funding from SNK and renamed itself Yumekobo, under which name it made Blazing Star in 1998. Three of the games in this series came out of the same building.

Sammy is the other half of the credit, and this is the only time it appears on a Neo Geo game. We checked that rather than repeating it: in MAME’s Neo Geo driver source, the only entries whose manufacturer field contains Sammy are viewpoin and its prototype set viewpoinp — the same game twice — and MAME’s Neo Geo CD software list has no Sammy titles at all. The credit MAME records is “Sammy / Aicom”. Wikipedia says the same thing in its own words: Viewpoint is Sammy’s sole Neo Geo MVS release.

That is a strange fact about a company that was enormous. Sammy was an arcade and amusement giant that would eventually merge with Sega; on SNK’s hardware it published exactly one game, and it is this one. The Japanese cartridge carries the copyright line ©SNK/サミー工業 — SNK and Sammy Industries together — which is as close as the object itself gets to explaining the arrangement.

Which release is the Japanese cartridge, and what is its code?

NGH-051, and unlike the last entry in this series there is no digit trap here. Neo Geo home codes were three digits until the late 1990s, when SNK switched to four; 1992 is firmly in the three-digit era, and we confirmed it the blunt way — searching Suruga-ya for a four-digit NGH0510 returns nothing at all.

The code comes from three independent sources that agree: Suruga-ya’s 型番 field on the cartridge’s product page reads NGH-051, the Japanese catalogue Pico Pico Daihyakka gives 型番：NGH-051, and satakore’s Japanese AES page lists the same reference. MAME is no help this time — its viewpoin entry records the manufacturer and the year but no product code — and VGCollect, which supplied a barcode for Blazing Star, has this title with “Item Number: NA”.

The disc is the interesting one. The Neo Geo CD release of 25 February 1995 has product code AICD-051, and that code is not something anyone had to reconstruct: it is sitting in the Agency for Cultural Affairs Media Arts Database as a schema:productID, which is rare for Neo Geo titles. MAME’s Neo Geo CD software list gives the same serial. AICD is Aicom’s own house prefix — the same pattern as ADK’s ADCD numbers — and the database’s copyright fields explain why: the disc carries both ©SAMMY 1992 and REPROGRAMMED ©AICOM 1995. The developer rebuilt its own three-year-old game for the CD hardware and put its own catalogue number on the result.

Two dates need care. The Media Arts Database dates the arcade release to October 1992 while Wikipedia gives 20 November 1992, so this guide says only “October 1992” for the board and leaves the day out. The cartridge date is not in dispute: 11 December 1992, in the database and on Suruga-ya’s product page alike.

What did it cost, and what does it cost now?

25,000円 before tax, and for once three sources say so together: the Media Arts Database records 25000円 (税抜), Pico Pico Daihyakka records 25,000円, and Suruga-ya’s 定価 field reads ￥27,500, which is the same figure with today’s 10% tax. When the numbers line up like this we print them.

Now, the cartridge and the disc have gone in opposite directions. On 20 September 2026 Suruga-ya had no copy of the cartridge to sell and was offering ￥180,000 to buy one from you. On the same site the Neo Geo CD version was in stock, graded 美品, at ￥6,370 — against a list price of ￥6,380, so a nearly-mint thirty-one-year-old disc was selling for ten yen less than it cost new. Its trade-in price was ￥3,400.

That gap of more than twenty-five times between the cartridge and the disc is the single most useful thing in this guide. It is the same six stages either way.

Why was there never a sequel — and what happened in 2025?

There was going to be one, and this time we know its name, its platform and the date it was supposed to come out.

Viewpoint 2064 was announced for the Nintendo 64, developed by Sammy with Racdym. It was shown at Nintendo SpaceWorld 1999 and, according to Time Extension, at the Tokyo Game Show, and it previewed well — IGN64 among others covered it warmly. The design took the original’s idea and made it switchable: the classic isometric view and a behind-the-ship view, across fifteen areas with branching routes, in the shape Star Fox 64 had made popular. The release date was set for 11 November 1999.

It missed it. The game was delayed repeatedly through 2000 and then quietly cancelled in 2001. The reason given is unglamorous: Sammy had development problems. So Sammy’s one Neo Geo game stayed a one-off, and the sequel joined the long list of N64 titles that exist only in preview screenshots.

Except that it did not stay there. A playable prototype surfaced on eBay in 2015, almost finished and completable. And then, on 24 November 2025, the preservationist Hard4Games dumped a final “Master” build from a developer cartridge and uploaded it to the Internet Archive — a version that fixes what the earlier prototype was missing, including collision detection, the advertised interface and the missing boss fights.

Every other entry in this series ends with an absence. This one ends with a file. The sequel to Viewpoint was announced for a Thursday in November 1999, cancelled, and became playable in November 2025 — twenty-six years late, for free, from the people who refuse to let this stuff disappear.

Can you actually buy the AES cartridge today?

Yes, and this is the easiest one in the series so far.

Suruga-ya, 20 September 2026: no cartridge, but plenty of everything else. The shop’s own stock of the AES cartridge (product 163000039) was 品切れ. Ask it to show sold-out items as well and the title returns 43 listings — cartridges in every condition grade, discs, arcade boards, instruction sheets, a manual on its own. That is a game that passes through the shop constantly, not one that never appears. And the Neo Geo CD version was actually in stock at ￥6,370.

Buyee, the same day: eleven listings for ビューポイント ネオジオ, and every single one of them is this game — one AES cartridge, seven Neo Geo CD copies, and three pieces of MVS paperwork. Not one unrelated item. That is the cleanest search result this series has measured on any shop, and it is a straight reversal of the last entry, where the same kind of search returned nothing relevant at all.

eBay, the same day: Viewpoint Neo Geo returned 103 results; on the first page of sixty, forty-seven are this game, including eight AES cartridges priced from about ￥198,000 to about ￥549,000, four MVS kits, and around thirty Neo Geo CD copies. The misses are mostly merchandise — two soundtrack CDs, three marquees, a poster, a magazine advert, a reproduction box and a mug.

So what can you actually get? Almost anything you want, at wildly different prices. The disc is ￥6,370 in Japan and the cartridge is ￥200,000-plus abroad. The honest advice is to decide first whether you want to play Viewpoint or own the 1992 object, because the difference between those two answers is about two hundred thousand yen.

How do you search each shop for it?

Suruga-ya: use the code, and expect sold-out listings. The link below opens NGH051 with sold-out items included, and it returns three listings, all of them this cartridge. If you type the code in yourself, switch sold-out items on, because the cartridge is out of stock and the default view hides it. The hyphenated NGH-051 is worse — seven listings of which only three are this game, the rest being Andro Dunos and NAM-1975, because the shop splits at the hyphen and matches “051” against other codes. For the disc, AICD051 and AICD-051 both return four listings and all four are this game. The Japanese title ビューポイント returns two things worth buying today: the disc and an overseas Genesis copy at ￥26,100.

Buyee: use the title with the platform. ビューポイント ネオジオ, eleven listings, every one of them this game. The product code does not work at all here — NGH-051 and NGH051 both return nothing — which is the usual pattern: Buyee searches sellers’ free text, and Japanese sellers write the title, not the code.

eBay: use the English title with the platform. Viewpoint Neo Geo is the search. Do not search Viewpoint AES on its own: it returns fifteen results of which only nine are this game, because the Korean web novel Omniscient Reader’s Viewpoint has a large English-language market and its light novels come up alongside a film camera. The product code is useless here too — NGH-051 returns zero.

Read the titles, not the counts. ROMカセット and AES are the home cartridge; CD, NCD, CD-ROM and スピンカード are the 1995 disc and its packaging; MVS, 基板 and インスト are arcade hardware and its paperwork; Genesis or メガドライブ is the 1994 overseas conversion, a different and much cheaper thing; and OST, marquee and poster are not the game at all.

Why search Kataban for this one?

Because two names work and two releases are missing, and it is better to know which is which before you start reading listings.

Checked in a desktop browser on 20 September 2026, ビューポイント returns two results and Viewpoint returns the same two: the 1992 Neo Geo cartridge and the 1995 Neo Geo CD disc. Both rows carry the English title, so either language gets you there.

Two is not the whole story, and we would rather say so. A Genesis version came out in December 1994 and a PlayStation version in January 1996, and neither has a row in the Media Arts Database, so neither appears in this catalog. The database is a record of Japanese releases as catalogued by the Agency for Cultural Affairs, and its coverage of this title stops at the two Neo Geo formats. If you are hunting the Genesis cartridge specifically, the catalog will not help you; the Japanese title search at Suruga-ya will.

The product code is a way in as of today. The database row for the cartridge carries no code of its own, so the field was filled in here from the three sources named above, and because the catalog strips punctuation before it matches, NGH-051 and NGH051 both land on the cartridge. The disc needed no such help — its AICD-051 came from the database itself. On a Japanese shop shelf, the kana to look under is ヒ.
