Vampire: Master of Darkness on Game Gear: the Castlevania Sega Never Made, and the Only Sega List That Knows Its Code

2026-10-31

  • Game Gear
  • Platformer
  • 1990s
  • SIMS

Vampire: Master of Darkness went on sale in Japan on 23 October 1992 as a Game Gear cartridge at 4,500円 before tax, under a Japanese title written in Latin letters: IN THE WAKE OF VAMPIRE. It was developed by SIMS with Sega CS and — this is the part that matters for everything below — published in Japan by SIMS, not by Sega.

This is an entry in a series about games that never got a second part. It is also the first entry where the publisher is not Sega, and that single fact broke two of our normal research steps and one of our normal buying steps.

It is a Castlevania game in everything but licence. You play a Victorian psychologist hunting a blood-draining killer through 19th-century London, you break scenery to find weapons, you throw daggers and stakes with up-plus-attack, and at the end of it there is Dracula. English Wikipedia puts the reason for its reputation plainly: the Castlevania series “was yet to appear on a Sega console and Master of Darkness was the closest — in terms of quality and gameplay — that the Master System had come to replicating the experience.”

  • Japanese title: IN THE WAKE OF VAMPIRE (read インザウエイクオブバンパイア), developed by SIMS and Sega CS, published by SIMS
  • Game Gear cartridge: 23 October 1992 · 4,500円 before tax · 2 megabits · one player
  • Catalogue number: T-44047 — a T- code, not a G- one, because Sega did not publish it
  • Five rounds, thirteen stages: four melee weapons, four throwables, lives plus a health bar, and a timer
  • Abroad: North America June 1993 and Europe 1993, both as catalogue number 2437
  • A Master System version exists — but not in Japan: Europe and Brazil only, and in the UK it came out in December 1992, six months before the Game Gear version
  • No sequel: only the Nintendo 3DS Virtual Console, 27 June 2012 in Japan and 4 July 2013 in the West
  • Buying it: Suruga-ya wants the Latin title, Buyee wants the katakana. On 21 September 2026 the shop’s own copy was ￥25,900 — the highest price in this series — against ￥8,360 on the same page

What is Vampire: Master of Darkness, and how does it play?

You are Dr. Ferdinand Social, a young psychologist who also researches the paranormal. Japanese Wikipedia sets the scene: in 19th-century London, on nights of the full moon, people are being found with every drop of blood drawn out of them, and the city has taken to calling whoever is doing it a vampire. Social decides something beyond human understanding is involved and starts investigating — and on one full-moon night a message appears on his ouija board naming the culprit as Dracula.

From there the route is a tour of gothic set pieces: the Thames, pursued by ghosts and thugs; a waxwork museum and a poltergeist; a cathedral full of skeletons and cultists; a strange laboratory in a castle in the woods; and finally Dracula himself.

The structure is five rounds of three stages each, except the fifth, which has one — thirteen stages in total, with a boss at the end of every round. You have lives and a health bar and a timer: you lose one when your hit points reach zero, when you fall into a pit, or when the clock runs out, and when the lives are gone it is game over.

The moment-to-moment is Konami’s, openly. One button swings your melee weapon, two jumps, and up-plus-one throws your sub-weapon. Stairs are climbed with up and down. You break masks scattered around the stage to make items appear, and certain walls give them up too. The melee weapons run knife (your starting kit, short and weak), cane, sabre (weaker but longer) and axe (short but heavy). The throwables are pistol and boomerang — sixteen shots per pickup — and dynamite and stakes at eight, the stake being the strong one that punches through enemies and terrain.

Which release is the Japanese cartridge, and what is its code?

The code is T-44047, and finding it took a source we had not used before in this series.

Start with what failed. The Media Arts Database has the cartridge as row M739915 — In the wake of VAMPIRE, Game Gear, 1992-10-23, publisher シムス株式会社, 4500円 (税抜) — with no schema:productID and no schema:gtin. Sega’s Game Gear software list, which supplied the code for the last three games in this series, does not contain this game at all: searching it for VAMPIRE, ヴァンパイア, バンパイア and WAKE returns nothing. And Suruga-ya’s trade-in page, which rescued the code for Ax Battler, prints 型番 as a bare dash.

The reason all three came up empty is the same one: Sega did not publish this game. The list we had been using is titled ソフトウェア一覧（セガ発売） — Sega-published software. There is a second list on the same site, ゲームギア対応ソフトウェア（ソフトライセンシー発売）, for everything published by licensees, and this game is in it:

10月23日 | In the wake of VAMPIRE | シムス | ACT | 4,500円 | T-44047 | 2M

That list runs to 92 rows, 59 of which carry a catalogue number, and — unlike its Sega-published sibling, which gives Ax Battler a number that belongs to a Donald Duck game — it contains no duplicates at all. The T-440xx block is SIMS’s own: T-44017, T-44027, T-44037, T-44047 and T-44057 are five SIMS titles in a row.

Two other sources agree. Sega Retro’s release table gives T-44047 for Japan. And Suruga-ya’s catalogue-number index returns this game, and only this game, for T44047 — even though the shop’s own trade-in page does not display the field.

One oddity worth knowing if you go looking yourself: only one of Suruga-ya’s five listings for this game carries the code at all, and it is a sold-out one. Search the number in the default view and you get nothing.

What was the list price, and what does it cost now?

Three sources agree on the list price: the Media Arts Database records 4500円 (税抜), Sega’s licensee list prints 4,500円, and Sega Retro gives ¥4,500. Suruga-ya shows 定価：￥4,950, the same figure with today’s 10% consumption tax. The number to quote is 4,500円 before tax.

What it costs now is the largest number in this series. On 21 September 2026, Suruga-ya had one copy in stock and the shop’s price was ￥25,900. On the same page, the marketplace line read ￥8,360 across four used copies.

So this is simultaneously the most expensive game we have covered and a roughly one-third saving sitting directly beneath the expensive number. For scale, the other Game Gear games in this series ran ￥12,100 / ￥2,830 for Ax Battler and ￥9,300 / ￥1,540 for Defenders of Oasis (prices as of 21 September 2026).

Why so much? Partly scarcity — four marketplace copies is the thinnest supply we have measured — and partly the Castlevania connection, which gives this cartridge a collector audience that a licensed Sega RPG does not have.

Was it any good?

The West and Japan disagreed, and it is worth reporting both.

Western reviewers liked it. English Wikipedia records that both the Game Gear and Master System versions “were well received critically, drawing particular praise for the detailed graphics and responsive controls”. Sega Force gave a composite 79% from two reviewers: Mat Yeo at 83%, praising the visuals and gothic atmosphere while finding some enemies annoying (“particularly the dog”), and Paul Wooding at 74%, calling the controls easy but the difficulty high and the play occasionally repetitive. Power Unlimited gave the Game Gear version 80%: “It starts slowly, but if you persevere you will find that Vampire: Master of Darkness is very varied. Add to that the creepy horror atmosphere with enough vampires, bats and voodoo, and you have a great platform game.”

Famicom Tsūshin’s four reviewers gave it 5, 6, 6 and 5 out of 10 — 22 out of 40.

That gap is not hard to read. In Europe, where the Master System stayed alive for years and Konami’s series never arrived on it, this was the closest thing available and was received as such. In Japan, where Castlevania had been on shelves since 1986 and everybody had played the real one, a competent handheld imitation scored like a competent handheld imitation.

Why was there never a sequel?

Nothing in the record explains it; there is only the absence. Neither the English nor the Japanese Wikipedia article names a sequel or a successor. The Media Arts Database has two rows for this title and no more: the 1992 cartridge and the Nintendo 3DS Virtual Console release of 27 June 2012.

We checked the two Sega download services where older properties were quietly kept alive — Sonic Cafe, the i-mode subscription service from 2001, and Game Toshokan, the Mega Modem service that distributed thirty-nine titles from November 1990. Searching both full title lists for Vampire, Darkness, Wake and SIMS returns nothing in either.

So: one cartridge, one Master System conversion that never came home to Japan, one Virtual Console release, and nothing since.

Can you buy it today, and which copy should you buy?

You can, but this is the thinnest market in the series. On 21 September 2026 Suruga-ya had one copy in stock plus four on its marketplace, and Buyee had four listings.

Suruga-ya holds five entries once you show sold-out items:

  • IN THE WAKE OF VAMPIRE — the complete one, in stock at ￥25,900, with marketplace copies from ￥8,360 (prices as of 21 September 2026)
  • …(状態：箱状態難※内箱含む) — complete, but the box is in poor condition. Sold out. This is the only row carrying the catalogue number
  • …(箱説なし) — cartridge only, no box or manual. Sold out
  • [外箱のみ]… — the outer box on its own. Sold out
  • [説明書のみ]… — the manual on its own. Sold out

The last two are not games. Read the bracket at the front of the title: 外箱のみ is a box, 説明書のみ is a manual.

Then there is the hardware problem, which is specific to this game. Suruga-ya also stocks two other things with almost the same name. Searching Master of Darkness returns four listings and none of them is the Japanese cartridge: two are MASTER of Darkness filed as 海外版マスターシステムソフト — the European Master System version, which was never sold in Japan — and two are 北米版 VAMPIRE：Master of Darkness, the North American Game Gear release.

The good news is that they never contaminate a Japanese-title search, because the Japanese and Western titles share no characters at all. Search IN THE WAKE OF VAMPIRE and all five results are the Japanese cartridge. Search the Western name and none of them are. There is no middle ground to filter, which is easier than Dragon Crystal, another Game Gear game from the same period, where the overseas Master System version turns up inside the Japanese search and has to be spotted by eye.

How do you search each shop for it?

This game has two correct search terms and they are not the same one, because the two shops index the Japanese title in opposite scripts.

Suruga-ya: type the Latin title. IN THE WAKE OF VAMPIRE returns one listing in stock and five with sold-out items shown, and all five are this game. Suruga-ya is generous here: the katakana reading インザウエイクオブバンパイア returns the same five, and so does a partial, differently-spelled ウェイクオブヴァンパイア. Anything close enough lands.

Buyee: type the katakana, in full. インザウエイクオブバンパイア shows results: 4 (this game: 4). The Latin title that works perfectly at Suruga-ya returns one listing here. And a partial katakana string — ウエイクオブバンパイア, dropping the leading イン — returns zero. Buyee is matching characters inside titles sellers typed themselves, and sellers typed the reading, in full.

That inversion is the thing to take away. For every other game in this series the two shops wanted the same word or a trivially different one. Here the shop that tolerates everything wants the printed title, and the shop that tolerates nothing wants the pronunciation.

Neither shop wants the catalogue number. At Suruga-ya, T44047 matches exactly one listing — correct, but sold out, so the default view shows nothing at all. T-44047 with the hyphen shows results: 3 in stock and 8 with sold-out shown (this game: 1), because the shop splits at the hyphen. On Buyee the code is worthless in both spellings: T-44047 shows results: 4 and T44047 results: 9, and none of them is this game — among them are cargo pants, a Toyota shim and a Nissan cable.

The Western title is worse than useless. Master of Darkness returns 37 listings on Buyee, none of them this game — they are records, many of them punk and metal, because “master of darkness” is a song title. At Suruga-ya it returns four listings that are all overseas hardware. That is now five games in a row in this series where the Western name fails in a Japanese shop, and the first where it actively returns the wrong object.

Why search Kataban for this one?

Because the catalogue is the one place where every name for this game works.

Checked in a desktop browser on 21 September 2026: IN THE WAKE OF VAMPIRE returns two results, the cartridge and the Virtual Console release. So does the mixed-case In the wake of VAMPIRE as the database writes it, and so does the katakana インザウエイクオブバンパイア. And so does Master of Darkness — the catalogue carries the English title, which is exactly the search that returns metal records on Buyee and Master System cartridges at Suruga-ya.

The catalogue number works here too, and only here. T-44047 and T44047 each return the cartridge — the Media Arts Database has no code for this row, so we added the one the licensee list supplies, on 21 September 2026. That makes Kataban the only one of the three places in this guide where the number finds the game: at Suruga-ya it matches a single sold-out listing, and on Buyee it finds cargo pants. The North American title with the colon works too: Vampire: Master of Darkness returns the cartridge alone (1 result), because since 1 October 2026 the catalogue also files it under that alternate English title. On a Japanese shop shelf, the kana to look under is イ.

Sega spent the Master System and Game Gear years without a Castlevania, and a licensee built one anyway, in London, with a ouija board and a psychologist. Europe treated it as the real thing; Japan gave it 22 out of 40. It got a Master System conversion it never brought home, a Virtual Console release, and no second part. If you want it, the label reads T-44047 — and the price you should be looking at is the second one on the page.
