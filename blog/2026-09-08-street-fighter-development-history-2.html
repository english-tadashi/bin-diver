Street Fighter Development History, Part 2: The Fire and the Hack Behind the 1991 Boom

2026-09-08

In Part 1, we covered how the original Street Fighter came out of a doodle at a boring meeting and shipped with special moves that were nearly impossible to throw on purpose. Its sequel didn't launch as a hit either. Street Fighter II's earliest location tests barely cleared 100 yen a day, well behind the shooting games it was tested alongside. What turned it into the fighting game boom of the early 1990s wasn't just good design — it was an illegally rewired arcade cabinet, a player nobody at Capcom could beat, and a night that nearly ended in a real fire. This is Part 2, told through the memories of the people who lived it.

  • Early location tests brought in only 98–99 coins a day, well below the 170–190 a solid shooting game managed at the time
  • An arcade employee's unauthorized two-monitor rewire is credited with sparking the head-to-head boom
  • An anonymous 85-win Zangief player at an Osaka arcade got the whole cast rebalanced out of fear, not imbalance
  • Composer Yoko Shimomura lowered a bag of master ROM data out of a third-floor window during a fire to make a factory deadline

Why Did Street Fighter II Almost Flop at Launch?

Before it became "the king of fighting games," Street Fighter II's early location tests told a different story. A solid shooting game of the era typically pulled in 170–190 coins a day. Street Fighter II was stuck at 98–99, unable to clear even the symbolic 100-coin line.

The turnaround came from outside Capcom entirely. Capcom's official CPS cabinet spec didn't allow two monitors to be wired together. One arcade employee did it anyway — pulling two extra leads from the board's harness to rig a face-to-face two-screen setup, so two players could compete without sitting side by side. That mattered more than it sounds: many Japanese players found it awkward to play shoulder-to-shoulder with an opponent, winning felt uncomfortable and losing felt worse. A booth where you could go all-out without watching each other's face changed everything. Combined with the arcade magazine Gamest relentlessly hyping the head-to-head format issue after issue, it exploded from a struggling cabinet into a nationwide craze.

Who Was the Legendary 85-Win Zangief Player?

At a game center called Chateau EX in Kyobashi, Osaka, a junior Capcom planner was doing routine location testing — stopwatch and notebook in hand, tracking how players actually used each character. In-house, Zangief's throws had been tuned to a reasonable strength.

Then one unnamed player showed up and started running through every challenger with Zangief, undefeated. The panicked call back to Capcom: "Zangief is too strong — he's on an 85-win streak, nobody wants to challenge him anymore!" Capcom sent three of its strongest in-house players to the arcade as reinforcements. The follow-up call was even more deflating: "We can't touch him. No chance."

The team nerfed Zangief the next day, and kept dialing him down over several sessions until even that unbeatable player stopped winning. Years later, Okamoto joked that the shipped, famously underpowered Zangief exists because of that one anonymous player at Chateau EX — the team overcorrected out of fear, not because Zangief was actually broken. Chun-Li suffered the same fate for the same reason: she tested as easy to pick up and won constantly in location tests, so she was quietly weakened before release too.

What Happened During the Fire in the Sound Room?

Once the game caught on, Capcom's culture flipped overnight — sales staff who'd spent months politely asking stores to take even one cabinet suddenly spent their days turning down orders they couldn't fill. Behind that glamour, the development floor was living on almost nothing: all-nighters were routine, male staff commonly slept on flattened cardboard under their own desks, and the sound team's handful of folding cots became the subject of nightly standoffs over who got one.

The most dangerous of those nights belonged to composer Yoko Shimomura. With a hard deadline — finished ROM data had to reach the factory by 7 a.m. — she was alone on the third floor finishing the music data when the exposed development board she was working from caught fire, filling the room with smoke. Normally she'd have evacuated immediately, but the building's night security was strict enough that opening an emergency stairwell after hours triggered an alarm and brought guards running, and floor-to-floor movement was restricted overnight. She was effectively locked in with the smoke.

At 5 a.m., director Ryo Nishitani realized what was happening and ran to the building below her window. Unable to get downstairs, Shimomura tied the finished ROM data — sealed in a convenience-store bag — to the end of a roughly 7-meter audio cable and lowered it out the third-floor window like a rope. Nishitani caught it, ran straight to the factory, and made the 7 a.m. deadline. That's the reason Street Fighter II's soundtrack made it into the game at all.

Why Is Ken Actually Stronger Than Ryu?

For years, players argued about whether Ryu and Ken were really identical. Okamoto always publicly said they were — that wasn't quite true. Ken is slightly stronger, and the reason is a leftover bug. Early in development, special moves like the Shoryuken had a vulnerability window built in — a moment of exposure afterward that could eat a big punish. When the team stripped this system out for balance reasons late in development, they removed it from Ken's data but simply forgot to remove it from Ryu's, leaving him with a marginally weaker Shoryuken than his palette-swap rival.

One more long-running mystery: every so often, a Hadouken flies out bright red for no visible reason. It's not a hidden command — lead programmer Uema built it in as a personal joke. The 256th fireball thrown since the machine powered on is forced to render red, with zero effect on damage or speed. A small, deliberate mystery, planted purely because he figured players would enjoy discovering it.

How Did Capcom Reward the Team Behind Its Biggest Hit?

Street Fighter II made Capcom an enormous amount of money. The team that built it did not see much of it. Okamoto repeatedly pushed his own superiors to raise pay for the people responsible — especially director Ryo Nishitani — and kept getting turned down. The final numbers: an average raise across the team of roughly 3%, and a special bonus for the game's lead director of ¥500,000.

The message landed hard: however much the game sold, it wasn't coming back to the people who made it, and there was no guarantee they'd get to make what they wanted next either. That disillusionment, combined with offers from overseas publishers, pushed several of Capcom's core creators — Nishitani among them — to leave and start their own studios, including what later became Arika.
