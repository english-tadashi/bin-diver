Super Potato Akihabara: What Is on Each Floor, and How to Find Your Game Fast

2026-11-22

Super Potato is the retro game shop most visitors to Akihabara already know by name, and the one they most often leave without the game they came for. It is not a hard shop to find. It is a hard shop to search: three floors, thousands of spines in Japanese, and no shelf label in English to tell you where your game is. This guide covers what is on each floor, and how to go from an English title to the right spine in a few minutes.

  • Where: Kitabayashi Building (北林ビル), 3F–5F, 1-11-2 Sotokanda, Chiyoda-ku. The shop is upstairs, so look for the sign rather than a shopfront.
  • Hours: 11:00–20:00, every day of the year.
  • Floors: 3F and 4F sell games and hardware. 5F is a retro arcade with a rest space and Japanese penny sweets (駄菓子).
  • Before you go: look your games up on Kataban and note each Japanese title and product code (型番). That code is printed on the cartridge or case, and it is the fastest way to confirm you have the right copy.

Address, hours, phone and floors are from Super Potato's Akihabara store page and the Chiyoda City Tourism Association's spot page, which agree on all four (checked 14 November 2026). The list of systems is the store page's own. Phone: 03-5289-9933.

What is on each floor of Super Potato Akihabara?

The shop occupies the top three floors of the building, and there is a lift between them. The third and fourth floors are the shop proper: domestic and imported games and consoles, priced individually. The fifth floor is not a sales floor. It is a retro game centre — old cabinets, a rest space and a counter of 駄菓子, the cheap sweets Japanese children bought after school. Super Potato's own page says you are welcome up there just to take a break.

If you only have twenty minutes, spend them on 3F and 4F. If you have an hour, finish on 5F: it is the easiest place in the building to sit down, check your list and decide what to buy.

Which systems does Super Potato stock?

More than most visitors expect. The store page lists Famicom, Super Famicom, Nintendo 64, GameCube, Game Boy, Game Boy Advance, Virtual Boy, Nintendo DS, PlayStation and PlayStation 2, Sega SG-1000, Mark III, Mega Drive, Mega-CD, 32X, Saturn, Dreamcast and Game Gear, WonderSwan, PC Engine (HuCard, CD-ROM² and Super CD-ROM²), PC-FX, Neo Geo and Neo Geo CD, Neo Geo Pocket, 3DO, MSX and Xbox — plus Game & Watch and other LCD games, game soundtracks, strategy guides and character goods. At the far end of the list are the machines collectors travel for: Atari Lynx and Jaguar, Epoch's Cassette Vision, Tomy's Pyuuta (ぴゅう太), Vectrex (sold in Japan as 光速船), Intellivision and Playdia.

Most of the software is the Japanese release. That matters for two reasons: the box art and title will often differ from the version you know, and the cartridge may not run on your console at home. Kataban's search result shows both — the Japanese title to look for, and whether that release works on North American and European hardware.

How do you find a specific game on the shelf?

Do the reading before you climb the stairs. Search the English title on Kataban and you get three things you can carry into the shop: the official Japanese title, the product code, and the title's first kana, which helps wherever a shelf is sorted by Japanese reading.

  1. Find the system’s section first. Narrowing to one console cuts thousands of spines to hundreds.
  2. Match the Japanese title. Hold Kataban's result next to the spines. You are matching shapes, not reading, and that is fast once you know what the title looks like.
  3. Confirm with the product code. Two games can share a title across systems or editions. The code on the cartridge label or case spine settles it.

If the game is not on the shelf, or it is behind glass, ask. Tap Show to shop staff on the Kataban result and the screen switches to the Japanese title, the product code and a ready-made question in Japanese — whether it is in stock, whether you can see the one in the case, whether it comes with box and manual. The staff member taps an answer on your phone, so neither of you has to speak the other's language.

Can you buy tax-free at Super Potato?

Super Potato's store page does not say. Ask at the register before you pay: the Show to shop staff screen in Kataban has a tax-free question ready in Japanese. If the answer is yes, bring your original passport, and note that since 1 November 2026 tax-free shopping in Japan works by refund — you pay the tax in the shop and get it back at the airport. Our guide to the new tax-free system explains the steps.

Is Super Potato enough on its own?

For browsing, yes. For buying a particular game at a particular price, rarely — it is one shop, and the same title can be cheaper, or simply in stock, a few minutes away. Super Potato is the first stop on our Chuo-dori walking route, which continues to Mandarake, Trader and Retro Game Camp. If you still have time after that, the chain-store crawl covers Suruga-ya, Hard Off and Book-Off, and Akihabara Deep Cuts covers BEEP.
