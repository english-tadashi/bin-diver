Mutation Nation on Neo Geo AES: SNK’s 1992 Beat-’Em-Up That Never Got a Sequel

2026-09-19

Mutation Nation is a beat-’em-up SNK put in arcades on 16 March 1992, and a month later it came home. On 17 April 1992 the same game went on sale as a Neo Geo AES cartridge, product code NGH-014, at 21,800円 before tax — the Japanese title spelled ミューテイション・ネイション on the spine.

This guide is part of a series about games that were good enough to earn a second part and never got one. Mutation Nation is a clean case: SNK kept the Neo Geo alive for another twelve years, the game charted in Japan, reviews were mostly kind — and no sequel was ever made. Everything that followed was the same 1992 game again, on a disc, on the Wii, on a download store. This guide covers what the cartridge is, what the code on it means, why two sources give it two different list prices, and how to search for a copy without buying the wrong format.

  • Japanese title: ミューテイション・ネイション, developed and published by SNK (株式会社エス・エヌ・ケイ)
  • Neo Geo AES (home cartridge): 17 April 1992 · product code NGH-014 · 21,800円 before tax, as recorded by the Media Arts Database
  • Arcade (Neo Geo MVS): 16 March 1992 · board number NGM-014
  • Two players at once: player one is Ricky Jones, player two is Johnny Hart, and the database records the cartridge as 1-2 Players (2人同時協力プレイが可能)
  • Neo Geo CD: 25 February 1995 · 5,800円 before tax — the same game on a disc, not a sequel
  • No second part: what followed were re-releases — the Wii Virtual Console, and Hamster’s ACA NEOGEO edition on 26 October 2017
  • One code, two spellings: Suruga-ya answers to NGH014 without the hyphen, Buyee answers to NGH-014 with it. The other way round, both get noisy

What is Mutation Nation, and who are you playing as?

The year is 2050. As the English Wikipedia article tells it, “a mad scientist’s superiors shut down his bizarre biological experiments”; “His lab later explodes, however, and the scientist himself mysteriously disappears.” What is left behind is loose: “A genetic virus has suddenly started spreading among the tenants, turning them into vicious mutants.” Two young locals come back to a home town that no longer looks like one: “Two young locals, Ricky Jones and Johnny Hart, return to town after having been away for a long time, and when they see the chaos that’s happening, they take it upon themselves to clean up.” You clean up with your fists.

Mechanically it is a side-scrolling brawler of the era: “One or two players (Player 1 as Ricky and Player 2 as Johnny) fight their way through the six levels of various mutated and mechanical enemies”, with “various combos, jumping attacks, throws and special moves”. The part that is its own is the sphere system — “the player can pick up one of four elemental spheres that allow the player the ability to unleash screen-filling super attacks.” Which sphere you are holding decides what your panic button does, so the pick-ups are a small resource to manage rather than a straight power-up.

It was not a flop. Wikipedia records “generally favourable reception from critics since its release in arcades and other platforms”, with scores collected there running from 2 out of 5 in AllGame to 92 out of 100 in HobbyConsolas and Joystick. It charted twice: RePlay reported it as the fifth most popular arcade game at the time, and Game Machine placed it sixteenth in its 1 May 1992 issue.

Which release is the Japanese cartridge, and what is its code?

Neo Geo games carry two numbers with the same digits: NGM- for the MVS arcade board and NGH- for the AES home cartridge. For this game the pair is NGM-014 and NGH-014, which is why MAME’s entry is titled “Mutation Nation (NGM-014 ~ NGH-014)”. The cartridge is the one a collector buys; the board needs an MVS cabinet or a consolised unit.

The Neo Geo Master List dates both the Japanese AES release and the English AES release to 17 April 1992, and the Neo Geo CD to 25 February 1995. So a US cartridge of the same game exists, and it turns up in Japanese shop searches as 海外版 — same game, different box and manual. Kataban’s catalog tracks the Japanese releases: row M757279 for the cartridge and M757280 for the disc in the Agency for Cultural Affairs Media Arts Database.

That database does not carry a product code for either row — no schema:productID, no schema:gtin. The code in this guide comes from three places that agree: the Master List, the MAME set title above, and Suruga-ya’s own 型番 field on the cartridge’s product page, which reads NGH-014. We added it to the catalog on 18 September 2026 from those three sources, so the card for the cartridge now shows the code.

Why do two sources give two different list prices?

Because one of them adds today’s consumption tax. The Media Arts Database records the cartridge at 21800円 (税抜) — tax-exclusive, as the database says. Suruga-ya’s product page for the same cartridge shows 定価：￥23,980. The two are the same figure: 21,800 × 1.1 = 23,980. The shop’s field applies the 10% rate in force today, not the 3% that existed when the game shipped in 1992.

So the number to quote is 21,800円 before tax, and the number you will see on the shop page is the same price with modern tax on top. Neither is what anyone pays now: on 18 September 2026 Suruga-ya’s own used copy of the cartridge was ￥230,000, a second copy graded 美品 (near-mint condition) was ￥273,700, and the trade-in price next to them was ￥125,000.

Why was there never a sequel?

The honest answer is that nobody has published one, and the record shows what came instead. SNK went on making Neo Geo games until 2004, and this game’s later appearances are all the same 1992 build moving to new hardware: the Neo Geo CD version on 25 February 1995 at 5,800円 before tax, then the Wii Virtual Console, then Hamster’s ACA NEOGEO release on 26 October 2017 for Nintendo Switch, PlayStation 4 and Xbox One — 823円 including tax on Switch and PS4, and reachable from a Japanese store account rather than a shelf.

Read the catalog and you can see the shape of it. Where SNK did build a series, it built it hard: the fighting games that started in 1991 and 1994 ran for a decade of numbered sequels. The beat-’em-up got one cartridge, one disc of that cartridge, and a re-release twenty-five years later. That is why it belongs in this series — not because it was ignored, but because being liked was not enough.

Where can you buy the Japanese cartridge today?

Suruga-ya: search the code without the hyphen. This is the one thing worth knowing before you start. On 18 September 2026, NGH014 returned exactly one listing, and it was this cartridge — the shop’s own two copies above, under one listing. The same code written the way the shop itself records it, NGH-014, returned ten listings of which only one was this game; the other nine were unrelated Neo Geo cartridges. Suruga-ya splits the code at the hyphen, so the hyphenless form is the precise one.

Buyee: search the code with the hyphen. Exactly the opposite. On the same day NGH-014 returned one listing on Buyee, a used AES cartridge, while NGH014 returned two — the cartridge plus a Yu-Gi-Oh card whose own code ends in 014. Buyee searches the seller’s free text, so a code with nothing to break it up drifts into other hobbies. The Japanese title with the platform, ミューテイション・ネイション ネオジオ, reported fourteen listings, of which four were this game and six were sealed Neo Geo CD copies of other SNK titles from one seller.

Read the titles, not the counts. Suruga-ya spells the home releases ミューティション・ネイション and its arcade items ミューテイション・ネイション — two spellings of the same name in one shop. The search engine treats them as the same word, so either spelling finds both, but the words that matter are the format ones: ROMカセット is the AES cartridge, CD-ROM is the 1995 disc, 基板 or インスト is arcade hardware, 説明書のみ is a manual on its own, and 海外版 is the overseas cartridge. Asking Suruga-ya to show sold-out items as well returned twenty-four listings on 18 September 2026, twenty-three of them this game in one format or another — which is the clearest picture of what exists, and a good way to learn the vocabulary before you bid.

Prices on both sites were what they were on the day we looked. A cartridge with one copy on the shelf moves in steps, not in a smooth curve, so treat the numbers above as a snapshot and not as a going rate.

Why search Kataban for this one?

Because the name is the way in, and there are three different things wearing it. Checked in a desktop browser on 18 September 2026, Mutation Nation returns two results — the 1992 AES cartridge and the 1995 Neo Geo CD disc — and ミューテイション・ネイション returns three, adding the 2017 download edition. Seeing them next to each other is the fastest way to know which one a listing is offering you.

The code is a way in as well. NGH-014 returns one result, the 1992 cartridge, and so does NGH014 without the hyphen — the catalog strips punctuation before it matches, so either spelling lands on the same card. That is a change from a day earlier: the Media Arts Database row carries no product code of its own, and the field was filled in here on 18 September 2026 from the three sources above.

A game can be reviewed well, chart in two magazines, sell for the price of a small appliance thirty years later — and still be the only one of itself. If you want the object, it is the 1992 cartridge with NGH-014 on the label: not the 1995 disc, not the overseas box, and not a sequel, because there is no sequel to confuse it with.
