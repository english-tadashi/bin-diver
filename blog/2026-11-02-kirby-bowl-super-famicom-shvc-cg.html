Kirby Development History, Part 2: Special Tee Shot in 1992, “Kirby’s Tee Shot” in 1994 — Kirby’s Dream Course and Its Product Code SHVC-CG

2026-11-02

  • Super Famicom (SNES)
  • Sports & racing
  • 1990s
  • Nintendo
  • Kirby

Kirby’s first Super Famicom game was not a platformer. カービィボウル (Kirby Bowl) came out in Japan on 21 September 1994, published by Nintendo, and reached North America in February 1995 as Kirby’s Dream Course. Kirby is the ball: you shoot him across a course, turn the enemies he hits into stars, and sink him in the cup. In Part 1, the story was a game that nearly came out under another name. This one is said to have started out as another game. This guide covers the golf game that came before it, what differs between the Japanese and American cartridges, and how to find the Japanese one by its code.

  • Japanese release: 21 September 1994 on Super Famicom, product code SHVC-CG — published by Nintendo, developed by HAL Laboratory with Nintendo
  • In 1992 HAL had a golf game without Kirby, Special Tee Shot (a build from that year survives); in December 1994 an American magazine still called this game Kirby’s Tee Shot
  • Eight courses of eight holes, and on each hole the last enemy left standing becomes the cup
  • Kirby can copy enemies’ abilities on the course, and it was the first Kirby game with a two-player versus mode
  • On 14 September 2026, Suruga-ya’s own used copies ranged from ¥540 to ¥2,250, and 23 of the 24 listings a Buyee search for SHVC-CG returned were this game on its own

Special Tee Shot and Kirby’s Dream Course: What Do the Sources Say?

In 1992, HAL had a golf game for the Super Nintendo called Special Tee Shot. SNES Central, a site that documents Super Nintendo prototypes and obtained a 1992 build of it, describes it as a planned early release by HAL that was eventually remade into Kirby’s Dream Course. The Japanese news site Inside Games told the same story in 2012: the game system is the same as カービィボウル, but without Kirby’s copy abilities or the がんばれ button, and the game was rebuilt around Kirby’s world.

Why it changed is less certain. Inside Games puts it down to making the game more fun and selling more copies; SNES Central suggests Kirby’s rising popularity may have been too good to pass up, and presents that as a guess. We could not find Nintendo or HAL telling the story themselves, so treat the reasons as unconfirmed.

The name changed in stages. Electronic Gaming Monthly’s December 1994 issue covered the game as Kirby’s Tee Shot, and SNES Central records the same working title. Special Tee Shot itself did reach players in the end: according to both SNES Central and Inside Games, it was broadcast on the Satellaview, the Super Famicom’s satellite service. Accounts of when it was first broadcast differ, so we leave the date out.

How Do You Play Kirby’s Dream Course?

Kirby is the ball. Enemies he hits turn into stars, and the last enemy left on the hole becomes the cup; sink Kirby in it and the hole is done, with your number of shots as the score. Each shot costs one point of vitality. The single-player game runs through eight courses of eight holes each, 64 in all, and clearing them leads to a showdown with King Dedede, according to Nintendo’s own Virtual Console manuals.

Two things make it a Kirby game rather than a golf game with Kirby on the box. Knocking out a glowing enemy gives Kirby its ability, which he can use while he is rolling; Nintendo’s original product page counts ten of them. And pressing the A button at the right moment while the ball is moving, the がんばれ (“hang in there”) button, makes it bounce more and travel a little further. The same Nintendo page calls the two-player mode the first versus mode in the Kirby series; in it, according to the Virtual Console manuals, two players take turns as a pink and a yellow Kirby and compete for stars.

What Changed Between Kirby Bowl and Kirby’s Dream Course?

The name, first: カービィボウル in Japan, Kirby’s Dream Course in North America, where Nintendo of America’s list of Super NES games dates it to February 1995. The cartridges differ as well. The Japanese ROM is 10 megabits and the American one 8, according to both the ROM database superfamicom.org and the fan encyclopedia WiKirby. The Cutting Room Floor, a site that documents unused and region-specific game content, points to one difference you can see: the Japanese version opens with a story scene, which the international versions moved into the instruction manual.

What Is SHVC-CG and How Do You Search for It?

SHVC-CG is printed on the cover of the Japanese instruction manual, next to the words 取扱説明書 (instruction manual). It is also the code the Japanese government’s Media Arts Database records for the game, taken from the cartridge label.

On Suruga-ya, search the short form. On 14 September 2026, SHVC-CG returned three listings, all カービィボウル, while SHVC-CG-JPN returned nothing. Suruga-ya’s own used copies were priced from ¥540 to ¥2,250. This time the code search also found a loose cartridge with no box or manual, at ¥1,180; in Part 1, a listing like that only turned up under the Japanese title. What the code search missed that day was a manual sold on its own, at ¥260, which only the title and barcode searches found.

On Buyee the code works as well. On the same day, SHVC-CG returned 24 listings; 23 were this game on its own, and the other was a three-game Kirby bundle. Searching カービィボウル スーパーファミコン instead returned 181 listings, and about seven in ten of the first 100 were this game on its own; most of the rest were bundles. Auction listings change every day, so treat these as that day’s numbers.

Where Else Can You Play It?

In Japan the game came to the Wii Virtual Console on 24 April 2007, the Wii U Virtual Console on 8 May 2013 and the New Nintendo 3DS Virtual Console on 7 June 2016, and it was in the first Super Famicom line-up on Nintendo Switch Online in September 2019. The Kataban catalog lists the Wii and 3DS releases as separate entries, with no product code. It is also one of the 21 games on the North American Super NES Classic Edition (2017), but not on Japan’s Nintendo Classic Mini Super Famicom, whose line-up leaves it out. Like the first game, it was also sold through the Nintendo Power rewriting service, and a rewritten memory cartridge is not SHVC-CG.

Any of the digital releases is the easy way to play it. If it is the 1994 cartridge you want, look for SHVC-CG.
