Rez on Dreamcast: Sega’s 2001 Music Shooter, Its Code HDR-0178, and Why It Never Got a Second Part

2026-11-24

  • Dreamcast
  • Shoot 'em up
  • 2000s
  • Sega

Rez — read レズ in Japan — is a music shooting game that Sega released in Japan on 22 November 2001 for the Dreamcast, product code HDR-0178, at 6,800円 before tax by Sega’s list, and for the PlayStation 2 on the same day. You fly through a wireframe computer network shooting down viruses, and every shot lands on the beat, so that playing well builds the music. It was made by United Game Artists, led by Tetsuya Mizuguchi.

This is the fifth and last entry in a series about Dreamcast games that never became a series. There was never a Rez 2. English Wikipedia says a sequel was being planned at Sega before its restructuring; instead, Mizuguchi made Child of Eden at another company, and Rez itself came back twice in new versions. Neither is a second Rez, and we explain why below. At the end of this guide we look back at all five Dreamcast games in the series.

  • Japanese title: Rez (レズ), published by Sega, developed by United Game Artists
  • Dreamcast GD-ROM: 22 November 2001 · 6,800円 before tax · HDR-0178 — the Media Arts Database row gives the date and the code, and Sega’s own Dreamcast list gives the same date, code and the price
  • PlayStation 2: the same day, SLPM 62101; English Wikipedia calls it Sega’s first game on that console
  • Later versions: Rez HD (Xbox 360, 2008) and Rez Infinite (2016 onwards)
  • No sequel: its spiritual successor Child of Eden (2011) came from Q Entertainment, published by Ubisoft
  • Buying it: the code at Suruga-ya, and the title plus ドリームキャスト on Buyee

What is Rez, and how does it play?

Both Wikipedias describe a rail shooter built on Panzer Dragoon’s controls: you lock on to several enemies, fire, and the sounds of each lock and hit are percussion in the track. Japanese Wikipedia says the music grows richer as you advance through the layers of each area, and that the whole design aims at synaesthesia — sight, sound and, through vibration, touch. English Wikipedia counts five areas and ten layer levels in each. For the PlayStation 2 there was a separate vibration unit, the Trance Vibrator; the Dreamcast version uses the controller’s vibration pack.

English Wikipedia says Mizuguchi conceived it in 1994 and 1995 from European club music, that production began in 1999 after Space Channel 5, and that the wireframe levels were inspired by Kandinsky’s paintings; the PlayStation 2 version was added during development as Sega moved to making games for other consoles. It sold poorly but was praised, and English Wikipedia says it is remembered as one of the Dreamcast’s best games. These are the sources’ descriptions; we have not played through the game to check them.

Is Child of Eden a sequel to Rez?

Not in the sense this series uses. English Wikipedia calls Child of Eden (2011) a spiritual successor, built on the same gameplay and sensory principles, and Japanese Wikipedia says it shares the concept, the controls and the world. But it was made by Q Entertainment, Mizuguchi’s later company, and published by Ubisoft — not by Sega, which owns Rez. A game in the same spirit by another company is not a second part. English Wikipedia adds that Mizuguchi had imagined Rez as the first of a trilogy of similar games, and that a Rez sequel had been planned at Sega before its internal restructuring; neither came to be.

Are Rez HD and Rez Infinite new games?

No. Rez HD is the game ported to the Xbox 360 by Q Entertainment and HexaDrive and released on Xbox Live Arcade in 2008. Rez Infinite, from Enhance Games and partners, first released in 2016, adds virtual-reality support and a new zone, Area X, which English Wikipedia says critics called the closest thing to a true sequel. It is still the 2001 game, expanded and re-released, so for this series it does not count as a second part. Sega’s Dreamcast list links the Rez row to Rez Infinite on the PlayStation Store as a リメイク・移植版 (remake and port).

Why was there never a Rez 2?

We could not find a reason given beyond what English Wikipedia says: a sequel was being planned at Sega, and Sega’s internal restructuring came first. We will not add to that. The Media Arts Database has one Dreamcast Rez row and three PlayStation 2 rows, and none of them is a second game.

Why search Kataban for this one?

Because a three-letter title finds too much. At Suruga-ya, Rez brings up the PlayStation 2 editions, the box with the Trance Vibrator and unrelated games; on Kataban it returns 32 rows, because the letters appear inside other titles. The code is the way in: HDR-0178 returns this disc alone, and SLPM 62101 the first PlayStation 2 edition. The katakana レズ returns nothing on Kataban, which lists the game under Rez. In second-hand game shops in Japan, such as Book-Off, games are often shelved in Japanese syllabary order, and this one is under レ.

A shooting game that plays like a track, a sequel planned and dropped, and a successor made elsewhere: this disc was never followed by a second one. If you want the Dreamcast original, the code is HDR-0178.

What did the five Dreamcast games have in common?

Each of the five stopped after one game, at the end of Sega’s last console:

  • Cool Cool Toon (T-3106M, 2000) — its English version cancelled as SNK went under
  • Napple Tale (HDR-0079, 2000) — one budget reprint with the same code
  • Segagaga (HDR-0151, 2001) — a game about saving Sega, one stage of which went to phones
  • Cosmic Smash (HDR-0176, 2001) — reimagined twenty-two years later by other companies
  • Rez (HDR-0178, 2001) — a sequel planned at Sega and dropped, a successor made elsewhere

Three more Dreamcast games that never got a second part appeared on this blog before the series began: Blue Stinger (HDR-0003, 1999), Maken X (T-14401M, 1999) and Eternal Arcadia (HDR-0076, 2000).

Reprints, ports, spin-offs and remakes — but none of the five was followed by a second part from the company that made it. That is the Dreamcast edition of this series. On 25 November we end with a one-off that went the other way: Flying Power Disc on the Neo Geo, a game whose sequel did arrive, thirty years later.
