Cleaning and Repairing Japanese Cartridges: Dirty Contacts, Save Batteries, and When to Pay a Shop

2026-11-24

You have bought a Japanese cartridge and it is home with you. Two things go wrong with old cartridges more than anything else: the game will not start because the contacts are dirty, or it starts but forgets your save because the battery inside has run down. This guide covers what you can do yourself, what you need, and when it is easier to let a shop do it. It is about fixing a game you already own; for what to check before you buy, see our guide to dead save batteries, out on 21 December 2026.

  • Game will not start: clean the contacts with isopropyl alcohol and a cotton swab; do not blow into the cartridge
  • Save is forgotten: the battery needs replacing, and replacing it erases the old save
  • Doing it yourself: a security bit to open the shell, a battery with solder tabs, and usually a soldering iron
  • Not doing it yourself: Japanese repair shops replace batteries, and many listings say 電池交換済み (battery replaced)

Why shouldn’t you blow into a cartridge?

Because your breath is wet. Epilogue, which makes cartridge readers for Game Boy and Super Nintendo games, says blowing “moves dust around and adds moisture from your breath”. iFixit says the same moisture promotes corrosion, and that Nintendo’s own troubleshooting page for NES Game Paks was updated to tell players not to blow into their Game Paks or systems. The habit seemed to work, iFixit explains, because taking the cartridge out and putting it back in made a better contact; the blowing itself did nothing for dirty or corroded contacts.

How do you clean the contacts?

With high-strength isopropyl alcohol and a cotton swab. The two sources differ on the strength: Epilogue asks for 99%, saying lower strengths “leave too much water on the contacts”, while iFixit says 90% or more, for the same reason. Either way, do not use water.

  • Dampen a cotton swab with the alcohol and wipe the row of contacts at the bottom of the cartridge.
  • Repeat with fresh swabs until a swab comes away mostly clean. Epilogue says two to four passes is normal, and warns against scrubbing hard, which can damage the plating.
  • Let the cartridge dry for a few minutes before you put it in the console.
  • If the contacts are oxidised and alcohol is not enough, both sources name DeoxIT, a contact cleaner. Epilogue adds that it leaves a thin film that slows the contacts from oxidising again.

Epilogue also suggests a soft white vinyl eraser for visibly corroded contacts, used sparingly because it removes some of the gold, and never a pink pencil eraser. That advice is Epilogue’s alone; we did not find a second source for it.

Which battery does each system use?

A lithium coin cell, but not the same size everywhere, so read the code printed on the old battery before you buy a new one. Epilogue says that on Game Boy boards the type is also printed on the board above the battery. Here is what our sources name:

For the Game Boy, the disagreement is the reason to check the cartridge itself rather than a list. Later Game Boy Advance games save without a battery, Epilogue says, so they have nothing to replace.

What do you need to replace it yourself?

  • A security bit to open the shell. Game Boy cartridges use a 3.8 mm bit, according to both iFixit and Epilogue. Racketboy’s Super Nintendo guide uses a 3.88 mm bit; we found only that one source for Super Nintendo cartridges and none for Super Famicom ones. Epilogue says Game Boy Advance cartridges take a tri-wing screwdriver, also a single source. Game*Spark opened its Famicom cartridge by releasing the plastic clips with a flat-head screwdriver.
  • A new battery with solder tabs. The original is soldered to the board. Epilogue says replacement “requires soldering skills or tabbed batteries” and advises a battery with tabs rather than soldering onto a bare coin cell; iFixit’s Game Boy guide desolders the old battery’s tabs and solders the new one’s in place.
  • A soldering iron and solder, unless you use a battery holder or tape. Racketboy, Game*Spark and a Super Famicom write-up by Paul O’Leary McCann each held the new battery in place with tape instead of solder.

Japanese Wikipedia says users are not advised to replace the battery themselves and that, in principle, the job goes to the maker. For games this old, the services we found are independent repair shops rather than the publishers.

Will a new battery erase your save?

Yes, in practice. Japanese Wikipedia says that taking the battery out to replace it stops it from keeping the save, so the data is lost. Game Repair Kobo’s Tenjin Chikagai shop in Fukuoka puts it the same way in its own notes on Game Boy repairs: the save is basically erased the moment the battery is removed. Racketboy’s guide warns you will lose your saves “no matter what”.

If you want to keep a save, copy it off first with a cartridge reader that can back up saves; Epilogue’s instructions say to back up with its own reader and to swap the battery quickly. A cartridge you bought second-hand usually has someone else’s save on it anyway. Even a sealed copy can have an old battery: our condition rank guide notes that unopened does not mean the battery is fresh.

Can a Japanese shop do it for you?

Yes. Epilogue notes that many retro game shops offer battery replacement, and in Japan it is a listed service. Otakaraou replaces batteries in Famicom, Super Famicom, Game Boy, Game Boy Color, Game Boy Advance and Nintendo 64 games by post only, at 1,000 yen per game including labour and tax (as of 8 November 2026), and says it does not guarantee the save. Game Repair Kobo’s Tenjin Chikagai shop lists Game Boy cartridge battery replacement from 3,000 yen (as of 8 November 2026). Both are domestic services, so from outside Japan you would need a Japanese address or a proxy to send the cartridge in and back.

The simpler route from abroad is to buy a copy that already has a new battery. Japanese sellers mark these 電池交換済み or セーブ電池交換済み, and Otakaraou’s own shop lets buyers add battery replacement as an option on some games.

Avoid listings marked ジャンク (junk) if you want a working save: they are sold as-is, with no promise that the game starts, let alone saves. For more on the word, see our junk guide.

Where can you buy a battery-replaced copy or the parts?

On 8 November 2026, Buyee showed 2,412 results for 電池交換済み スーパーファミコン and 2,719 for 電池交換済み ゲームボーイ, and eBay showed 79 results for CR2032 solder tabs and 604 for 3.8 mm security bits. Buyee’s search also matches listings that contain only some of the words, so read each title.

To find a battery-replaced copy of one particular game, look up its Japanese title on Kataban first, then search Buyee for that title followed by 電池交換済み. For example, Kataban gives the Japanese title of Pokémon Red, a Game Boy game that saves to a battery, as ポケットモンスター 赤, so the Buyee search is ポケットモンスター 赤 電池交換済み. On 8 November 2026, that search returned 77 listings, 34 of them this game on its own.
