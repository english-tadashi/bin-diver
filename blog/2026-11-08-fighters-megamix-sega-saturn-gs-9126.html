Fighters Megamix on Sega Saturn: AM2’s 1996 Crossover of Virtua Fighter 2 and Fighting Vipers, Its Code GS-9126, and Why There Was Never a Second Mix

2026-11-08

  • Sega Saturn
  • Fighting
  • 1990s
  • Sega

Fighters Megamix — ファイターズメガミックス, printed on the disc as FIGHTERS MEGAMiX — is a 3D fighting game that Sega released in Japan on 21 December 1996 for the Sega Saturn, product code GS-9126, at 5,800円 before tax. It was made by Sega AM2, the team behind Virtua Fighter, and it put the whole cast of Virtua Fighter 2 in the same game as the cast of Fighting Vipers.

This is the third entry in a series about Sega Saturn games that never became a series. Fighters Megamix was built from two other games, but nothing was built from it in turn: there was never a second Megamix, and the only other version is a handheld adaptation by another company. It also left Japan: it came out in North America and Europe in 1997 under the same name.

  • Japanese title: ファイターズメガミックス (disc and database: FIGHTERS MEGAMiX), published by Sega (セガ・エンタープライゼス), developed by Sega AM2
  • Sega Saturn CD: 21 December 1996 · 5,800円 before tax · product code GS-9126 — the Media Arts Database and Sega’s own Saturn software list agree on all three
  • What it mixes: the fighters of Virtua Fighter 2 and Fighting Vipers, with some moves from Virtua Fighter 3, plus hidden characters from other Sega games
  • Western releases: North America and Europe, 1997, as Fighters Megamix
  • Nothing after it: no second Megamix; a 1998 adaptation for Tiger’s Game.com handheld is the only other version, and we found no re-release of the Saturn game
  • Buying it: the title plus セガサターン on Buyee, and the code without the hyphen at Suruga-ya

What is Fighters Megamix, and how does it play?

Every Virtua Fighter 2 character and every Fighting Vipers character is playable from the start, and the two groups fight by each other’s rules. Both Wikipedias describe the main switch: the Fighting Vipers fighters keep their armour, which can be broken off, and the Virtua Fighter fighters gain moves that break it; you choose whether to fight in walled arenas, as in Fighting Vipers, or open ones without the ring-outs of Virtua Fighter. Japanese Wikipedia lists a dodge button that lets every character sidestep, taken from Virtua Fighter 3; English Wikipedia describes the same sidestep.

The other draw is who else is on the disc. Both Wikipedias list the same hidden fighters from other Sega games: Janet from Virtua Cop 2, the racing car Hornet from Daytona USA, which stands up on its back wheels to fight, the hero of the Mega Drive game Rent-A-Hero, and Siba, a character cut from the first Virtua Fighter before release, who makes his debut here. Deku, a bean in a hat, is the only character made for this game. English Wikipedia says the game sold more than 600,000 copies in Japan and that Famitsu gave it 35 out of 40; those two figures come from that one article.

How is it related to Virtua Fighter and Fighting Vipers?

It is made out of them, and the sources say so in different words. English Wikipedia calls it “a crossover between Sega’s 3D arcade games Virtua Fighter 2 and Fighting Vipers”. Japanese Wikipedia describes it as a festival-like game, お祭り的な要素, made to have the characters of the two games, which then split Sega’s fighting fans between them, appear together. Neither calls it a sequel to either game, and we do not either.

Its link to Virtua Fighter 3 is also worded differently. English Wikipedia says it was intended as an introduction to Virtua Fighter 3, which was announced for the Saturn but never released on it. Japanese Wikipedia says that Virtua Fighter 3 had just reached arcades and that there were plans to bring it to the Saturn, which is why some of its moves were added to the Virtua Fighter 2 characters. Both agree on the result: Virtua Fighter 3 moves in a Saturn game that Virtua Fighter 3 itself never reached.

The game was announced late. Both Wikipedias say it was revealed at a press conference less than two months before it went on sale. They give different dates for it, so we leave the exact day out.

Why was there never a second Megamix?

We could not find a reason given anywhere, so we will not offer one. What we can set out is what exists. The Media Arts Database has one Fighters Megamix row, the Saturn disc. English Wikipedia records one other version, an adaptation for Tiger Electronics’ Game.com handheld in 1998; for this series a port counts as the same game, not a new one.

Fighting Vipers did continue without it. Fighting Vipers 2 followed it (the Media Arts Database has its Dreamcast disc, HDR-0133, from 2001). Japanese Wikipedia notes that some of the moves of URA Bahn, a hidden Fighters Megamix character, were carried back into Bahn in Fighting Vipers 2. That is a borrowing in a sequel to Fighting Vipers, not a second Megamix; nothing we found continues the mix itself. English Wikipedia’s “Legacy” section cites later writers who call the game an early precursor of crossover fighting games — MeriStation in 2024 called it a spiritual precursor to Nintendo’s Super Smash Bros. — but that is other people’s games, by other companies.

Has it ever been re-released?

Not that we could find. Sega’s own Saturn software list links the games it has brought back, and the Fighters Megamix row has no link: just the date, the genre, 5,800円 and GS-9126. English Wikipedia’s list of Sega Ages releases does not include it, and its article on the game mentions no version after the Game.com one. Unlike Fighting Vipers, which the Media Arts Database shows on PlayStation 2 and PlayStation 3, Fighters Megamix has stayed on the Saturn.

How do you tell the Japanese disc from the Western ones?

The Western releases kept the name, so the title alone does not tell you. English Wikipedia dates the North American release to 15 May 1997 and the European one to 5 June 1997. A listing with “Japan”, “NTSC-J”, “spine card”, GS-9126 or 1996 is the Japanese disc; the Western discs are 1997. English Wikipedia also notes that two of the unlockable gallery pictures were changed in the American release and kept in the European one — a difference you will not see in a listing, but one reason some collectors want a particular region.

Because the shops file it under different spellings, and Kataban takes any of them. GS-9126, Fighters Megamix, the disc’s own FIGHTERS MEGAMiX and ファイターズメガミックス, with or without a space, each return this disc, with the Japanese title next to it to copy into a shop. Two casts in one game, a few guests from across Sega, and moves from a sequel the Saturn never got: Fighters Megamix was a one-time mix, and it stayed one. If you want it as it was sold in Japan in 1996, the code is GS-9126.
