Fire Emblem's DS and 3DS Remakes: Shadow Dragon, New Mystery and Echoes, and Which Japanese Copies to Buy

2026-09-17

Fire Emblem: Fortune’s Weave comes out today, 17 September 2026, on Nintendo Switch 2. It is the eighteenth main Fire Emblem game and the first since Engage in 2023 — which means a lot of people are about to play their first Fire Emblem, finish it, and wonder where the series came from.

The honest answer is the Famicom and the Super Famicom, where the first five games were made; we covered those in Fire Emblem: Where It All Began. But those cartridges are Japanese-only and more than thirty years old. Three later remakes rebuilt the oldest stories for handhelds: Shadow Dragon and New Mystery of the Emblem on the DS, and Echoes: Shadows of Valentia on the 3DS. This guide covers what each one kept, what each one changed, and how to find the Japanese copies without landing on the wrong version — including one that will not start on a Western 3DS at all.

  • Fire Emblem: New Mystery of the Emblem — ファイアーエムブレム 新・紋章の謎 〜光と影の英雄〜 · Nintendo DS · 15 July 2010 · Nintendo · box code TWL-P-VI2J (not in Kataban’s catalog) · Japan only · a remake of Book 2 of the 1994 Super Famicom Mystery of the Emblem

  • Region: DS cartridges are region-free on a DS or DS Lite. 3DS software is region-locked, so a Japanese Echoes needs a Japanese 3DS
  • The one to import: New Mystery of the Emblem is the only one of the three that never came out in English, so the Japanese cartridge is the only cartridge

What did Shadow Dragon keep from the 1990 original, and what did it change?

Shadow Dragon is a remake of Shadow Dragon and the Blade of Light, the Famicom game that started the series. The campaign is the same — Marth’s war across Archanea — and so is the rule Fire Emblem is known for: a unit that falls in battle stays gone.

What changed is mostly the interface and the safety net. The remake added displays for movement and attack range, the ability to change a unit’s class, more difficulty options and tutorials, and the weapon triangle that later games had made standard. It also added gaiden chapters that give you new recruits if too many of your original army have died — the game’s answer to permanent death, rather than an undo button. Online, players could fight each other with teams of five units and lend units to each other’s campaigns.

It is a milestone for English speakers, too: its release was the first time any version of the first Fire Emblem came out outside Japan. For importers that cuts both ways. The English cartridge exists, so the Japanese one is for collectors, for Japanese-language players, and for anyone who finds it cheaper — which, on the day we checked, it usually was.

What is New Mystery of the Emblem, and why is it the one to import?

Mystery of the Emblem on the Super Famicom was two games on one cartridge: Book 1 retold the Famicom original, and Book 2 was a new story set two years later. Shadow Dragon had just remade the game Book 1 retold. New Mystery of the Emblem remakes Book 2 — the Japanese Wikipedia article calls it a renewal of the second part.

It was not built on top of Shadow Dragon; according to Wikipedia, the staff started over from the original Mystery of the Emblem. The largest additions were an Avatar — a character you create, who fights beside Marth and was meant to give players an easier way in — and a Casual Mode in which fallen units come back, an option Wikipedia describes as the biggest point of contention among the developers. Episodes from BS Fire Emblem were remade as extra chapters under the name New Archanea Chronicles, and there were online battles, unit loans and an online shop.

It never came out in English. Wikipedia calls it the last Fire Emblem game to stay exclusive to Japan: the next one, Awakening, was the game that made the series popular worldwide. Of the three remakes, this is the only one where the Japanese cartridge is the only way to own it.

How did Echoes rebuild Fire Emblem Gaiden?

Echoes: Shadows of Valentia remakes Fire Emblem Gaiden, the 1992 Famicom second entry — the odd one out that our first guide described as early rather than wrong. It keeps what made Gaiden different: two protagonists, Alm and Celica, leading separate armies across Valentia, a world map you travel across, and dungeons you explore.

What it adds is the modern layer. It has full voice acting, a first for the series. Dungeons are explored in 3D. Mila’s Turnwheel lets you rewind turns, and a Casual Mode brings fallen units back after a battle. The story was expanded as well.

Japan had three editions: the standard release, a limited edition with a soundtrack, and a Valentia Complete edition that added an artbook to the limited-edition contents. All of them carry the same cartridge code, LNA-CTR-AJJJ-JPN, which is printed on the cartridge label. If the edition matters to you, go by the barcode instead: 4902370536430 is the standard edition and 4902370536447 the limited edition, according to Suruga-ya’s listings. We did not find a barcode for Valentia Complete.

There is also an English version, released in North America and Europe on 19 May 2017 — which matters because of the next question.

Will Japanese copies run on your console?

The two DS games: yes, on a DS or DS Lite. Every Nintendo handheld except the DSi and 3DS families is fully region-free, so a Japanese DS cartridge starts on a Western DS or DS Lite. Shadow Dragon is a standard DS card — its code begins NTR — and the region lock on the DSi and 3DS applies to DSi-enhanced and 3DS software, not to standard DS cards. New Mystery’s box code begins TWL instead. DSi-enhanced games are region-locked on DSi hardware, and we could not confirm whether New Mystery uses any DSi features, so an original DS or DS Lite is the safe choice for it.

Echoes: not on a Western 3DS. 3DS software is region-locked, and the lock applies to all of it. A Japanese Echoes cartridge will not start on a North American or European 3DS or 2DS. Buy the Japanese copy if you own a Japanese 3DS, or if you want it for the shelf; to play the game on a Western system, the English release is the one you need.

All three Japanese copies are in Japanese only.

How do you find the right Japanese copy of each game?

Search the way each shop indexes the game — the product code at one shop, the title at another — and never trust an English title on eBay without a region filter. The counts below were measured on 11 September 2026 in a desktop browser from a Japanese IP address. Stock changes daily.

Shadow Dragon

  • On eBay, the English title finds the English game. Searching Fire Emblem: Shadow Dragon nintendo ds returned 87 listings. The top result was a North American cartridge, and the first listing with “Japan” in its title was 23rd. Restricted to region code NTSC-J (Japan), the same search returned 15: eleven single Japanese copies, three multi-game lots and one unrelated game. The eBay link below applies that filter
  • Do not search eBay by the code. NTR-YFEJ-JPN and NTR-YFEJ both returned no results, even though two of the Japanese listings had the code in their titles
  • At Suruga-ya, search NTR-YFEJ, without the -JPN. That returned only this game: one copy in stock, four including sold-out listings. The full NTR-YFEJ-JPN returned nothing. Suruga-ya’s English-language site is not linked here: its search for the English title returned only New Mystery of the Emblem
  • On Buyee, the Japanese title returned 81 listings; 69 were this game, and most of the rest were bundles
  • On Kataban, search the code, or keep the dot: 新・暗黒竜 finds it, 新暗黒竜 without the dot does not

Fortune’s Weave is where a lot of players will start. If it sends you back, these three are the bridge: two you can play in English, one you can only play in Japanese, and all three still on the shelves in Japan. For the games they were built from, see the Famicom and Super Famicom originals; for what came between, the GBA trilogy.
