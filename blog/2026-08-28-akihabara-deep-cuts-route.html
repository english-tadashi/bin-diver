Akihabara Deep Cuts: Hidden Retro Game Shops

2026-08-28

Who This Walk Is For

This is the third Akihabara route, and it assumes you have already done the other two. If Chuo-dori was the famous shops and the chain walk was the volume play, this one is the leftovers — three shops that sit off the main strip, each with a reason to exist beyond “more shelves of the same games.”

Set expectations accordingly. Only three stops, none of them large, and it is entirely possible to walk all three and buy nothing. That is the nature of a deep-cuts route: you come here for the specific thing the big shops do not carry, not to browse.

The Route

From the Electric Town Exit, head west away from Chuo-dori. Trader2 is about 4 minutes out, on the quieter side of the district. From there the route swings north and east — roughly 5 minutes to Lashinbang AKIBA DEEP BASE, then 2 minutes across to BEEP. About 11 minutes of walking end to end, which makes it an easy add-on to a morning spent on Chuo-dori rather than a day of its own. BEEP sits a short walk north of the station, so the way back is straightforward.

Store-by-Store Notes

  • Trader2 — The second Trader branch, away from the flagship on the main street. Same chain, separate inventory, and noticeably less foot traffic. Worth the detour precisely because the tourists who hit Trader's main store rarely continue to this one, so the shelves get picked over more slowly.
  • Lashinbang AKIBA DEEP BASE — Lashinbang's deeper-stock branch, and the name is not marketing. Where the chain's regular shops carry the obvious things, this one leans into niche and doujin-adjacent material. If you are hunting something that never charted, this is a better bet than any of the general shops.
  • BEEP — The specialist stop, and the reason to do this route at all. BEEP deals in vintage Japanese computers and the software and parts that go with them — the PC-88, X68000, MSX side of retro that the console-focused shops largely ignore. It is in the basement, and the sign is mounted low near street level, so it is genuinely easy to walk straight past. Look down for the stairs rather than up for a storefront.

No Japanese? No Problem

This route is where the language gap actually bites, because the stock is obscure enough that you may not recognise a title even in English. For Famicom, Super Famicom, and other cartridge-era games, type the product code (型番) from the label into Kataban and you get the English title back. For PlayStation, Saturn, and Dreamcast discs, the barcode on the case works. Vintage computer software at BEEP is the one category where neither reliably helps — those boxes predate consistent barcodes, and the codes are not standardised the way cartridge codes are. Photograph the box and check it later rather than guessing at the shelf.

Practical Tips

Smaller independent shops are more likely to be cash-preferred than the chains on the previous route, so carry yen rather than assuming a card will work. Stock here also turns over slowly by design — if something is not on the shelf today, coming back next week is unlikely to change that, which is the opposite of how the chain stores behave.

Give yourself more time per shop than the size suggests. These are small rooms with dense shelves, and the interesting things are rarely at eye level.

Before You Go

Do this walk with a want-list, not with an open mind. The whole value of a deep-cuts route is finding the one specific thing the big shops never had, and that only works if you know what you are looking for. Look your titles up on Kataban first, note the product codes, and treat the three stops as a targeted search rather than a browse.
